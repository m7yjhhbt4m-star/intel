# Boat Harbour / A’se’k — Source Register and Evidence Quality

**Version:** 2.1 — 22 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Trace the evidence used in the comprehensive review and identify what each source can and cannot establish.

## Evidence labels

- **Primary regulatory source:** Government decision, assessment, monitoring or enforcement record.
- **Primary scientific source:** Peer-reviewed study reporting methods and results.
- **Project-proponent source:** Build Nova Scotia or consultant document; important but not independent verification.
- **Secondary reporting:** Media or synthesis; useful for leads/status, not a substitute for primary records.
- **Model output:** Prediction or scenario, not a measured environmental result.

## Source hierarchy and interpretation rules

1. Prefer original peer-reviewed papers and government records over secondary summaries.
2. Distinguish measured sample results from model predictions, project estimates and risk-assessment assumptions.
3. Do not merge sediment inventory estimates with waste-cell capacity or modelled suspended-solid volume.
4. Do not extrapolate a study beyond its sampled area, time period, media or analytes.
5. Preserve qualifiers, detection limits, dry/wet basis and guideline version.
6. When a source table is internally inconsistent or a number is figure-derived, flag it rather than silently repairing it.
7. A project-proponent report is valuable for project status but is not an independent environmental finding.

**IAAC's account of the Proponent's HHERA (not an independent re-performance of the HHERA):**
- The assessment considered soil, sediment, surface water, groundwater and country foods, and assumed potential post-closure residential use on land and recreational use (including country-food collection/consumption) in land- and water-based areas.
- The Proponent screened out soil, groundwater and surface water as human exposure pathways based on concentrations below screening guidelines or background; it retained direct contact with sediment and consumption of some country foods. Dioxins/furans and vanadium in sediment were associated with identified non-carcinogenic health risks for PLFN resident/recreational-user scenarios.
- The Proponent's HHERA treated consumption of plants, game organs and waterfowl as operable but treated terrestrial plants, fish from wetlands, shellfish, terrestrial game meat/organs and aquatic game meat as inoperable. Health Canada challenged the screening methods and COPC/pathway exclusions, raised bioaccumulation/biomagnification concerns, and recommended extending country-food monitoring beyond the estuary to address suspended sediment and potential transport into the Northumberland Strait.
- Health Canada and Nova Scotia Environment and Climate Change challenged exclusion of potable groundwater. IAAC agreed that the potable groundwater pathway should not have been deemed inoperable under the provincial contaminated-sites framework, even though the Proponent said no potable wells were currently used within the SSA. IAAC noted PLFN's concern that a potable-well exclusion zone could constrain future community expansion.
- Health Canada questioned the exposure-point-concentration method for delineating sediment-removal areas, noting that statistical exposure estimates do not precisely remove contamination and that hot spots may remain. IAAC stated uncertainty remained about whether the proposed SSTLs were fully protective; it considered residual contamination likely to remain in wetlands and said proposed SSTLs could reduce, but not eliminate, existing health risks.
- IAAC identified the possibility of post-remediation administrative controls, including access restrictions or country-food bans, if unacceptable residual risks were found. It stated remedial objectives should minimize the need for such controls.
- IAAC's final 2025 Decision Statement subsequently made pre-site-preparation HHRA reevaluation, consultation, country-food monitoring, and environmental-media follow-up legally binding through conditions 5.3–5.5. This must be distinguished from the Proponent's original HHERA conclusions.

### BH-012 expanded review note — BGC External Technical Review and 2019 Pilot Report evidence — 25 November 2026

The original BGC Engineering *External Technical Review – Final*, commissioned by IAAC and dated 29 January 2021 (document 141419E), was opened and reviewed in this pass. BGC's stated scope was two charge questions: (1) the Proponent's alternatives assessment and preferred waste-containment alternative; and (2) the robustness/efficacy of the proposed expanded containment cell. BGC states that it reviewed the 2019 GHD *Pilot Scale Testing Construction Report* (23 December 2019, 580 pages) as an extra-scope document requested during the review. The original 580-page GHD report itself remains inaccessible through the public Google Drive preview/download link in this pass; therefore, the following pilot-study details are attributed to BGC's quotations/summaries, not to a direct review of the original GHD report.

**Pilot-study findings reported by BGC:**
- Pumped slurry averaged 5.2% solids in Phase 1 and 2.4% in Phase 2, with values as low as 1–1.5%, against a 5% target. BGC noted that lower solids could extend Geotube dewatering periods and delay construction.
- In Phase 1, the first Geotube layer reportedly moved from 35% bulking to 28% overall volume reduction after four months (a 63% net volume change as described in the ETR). Geotube G6 was filled to capacity four times before its storage capacity was maximized. Differential dewatering/settlement was observed between bags.
- The report reportedly identified difficulty distinguishing sludge from underlying BHSL sediment during mechanical dredging with a CAT 320D excavator; the materials mixed during operation, creating an over-dredging risk and increasing the waste volume requiring storage.
- The pilot report included a temporary leachate treatment facility (TLTF) pilot. BGC described the results as promising, including treatment of bulk water using an organo-clay media step between filtration and adsorption to reduce long-chain organics such as TPH. BGC nevertheless considered the number of samples limited for extrapolation to approximately 1 million m³ of material and said full-scale compliance remained uncertain because site-specific discharge criteria were still being developed.

**BGC's independent conclusions and critical design issues:**
1. The existing containment cell expansion from 220,000 m³ to up to 1,073,000 m³ depended heavily on uncertain waste-volume reduction and Geotube consolidation. BGC identified a material risk that even the 143,000 m³ contingency could be insufficient; it recommended preserving/reconsidering a secondary on-site cell and/or off-site disposal as contingencies.
2. BGC found that the evidence did not demonstrate the stability of the 3H:1V or 4H:1V side slopes, the perimeter berms under Geotube/sludge loading, or the final cover over consolidating waste. The ETR raised potential geomembrane stress/cracking if long-term consolidation or differential settlement occurs.
3. The proposed 25 m storage height, end-dumped sludge between Geotubes, and placement/compaction of sand for the cover lacked sufficient construction detail. BGC questioned whether wet sludge could be compacted or contained without flowing beyond the berms, and whether end-dumped material could blind Geotube fabric and reduce dewatering.
4. BGC said the single underdrain sample from MH-1 used for the post-closure leachate model was not a robust dataset and did not reflect possible chemical changes from reagent dosing and dewatering effluent. The forecast leachate chemistry/quality and volume estimates could require revision.
5. BGC said the available documents did not establish a sufficiently detailed water sampling, monitoring and QA/QC program or the roles responsible for developing and implementing it.
6. BGC reported that the EIS's Point C water-quality improvement claim for 2020 was not accompanied by the underlying data in the ETR document set.
7. BGC noted that the cost estimates carried from the 2018 RODD to the 2020 EIS appeared unchanged despite refinements from pilot testing and the HHERA; economic comparisons used concepts at different design levels.
8. BGC could not assess the full range of alternatives removed during early workshops because those records were not provided. It also did not have the details of stakeholder input used to eliminate options.

**Limits on BGC's review:** BGC states it did not conduct a site visit, did not perform a detailed data-quality review, did not undertake detailed analytical studies, and did not review the HHERA itself. Extra-scope documents (including the Pilot Scale Testing Construction Report) were reviewed only to the extent needed to understand claims in the main scope documents.
