# Boat Harbour / A’se’k — 2025–2026 Status, Public Comment and Technical Review Findings

**Version:** 1.0 — 22 November 2026  
**Research status:** New primary-record and registry-page review; this is not a complete review of all registry comments or all EIS appendices.

## Executive finding

The project is not in a construction/remediation phase on the latest public status record located in this pass. Build Nova Scotia's March 2026 update says the project team is working with Pictou Landing First Nation (PLFN) to assess an alternative to the existing approved containment cell, while PLFN's judicial review of the January 2025 federal approval remains ongoing. The 2025 Annual Report (registry reference 74, dated 30 March 2026) is indexed as the proponent's compliance report for 24 January–31 December 2025; a secondary catalogue summary describes the project as pre-construction and says many conditions were awaiting the judicial-review outcome. The annual report PDF itself was not successfully retrieved and read in this pass, so that summary is not treated as a substitute for the report.

This changes the immediate independent-monitoring task: there is no basis to design a construction-phase audit as if dredging, Geotube operation, dam removal or post-closure monitoring were already underway. The near-term work is to secure the final monitoring plans, identify which baseline monitoring is being maintained during delay, obtain raw historical data, establish the independent monitor's governance and reporting arrangements, and ensure a future baseline is not stale by the time construction begins.

## 2. Environmental Assessment Report: material points to carry into the independent design

The January 2025 IAAC Environmental Assessment Report is directly accessible as HTML and was examined in the relevant sections below.

### 2.1 Groundwater — unresolved pathway and conceptual-model issue

IAAC records that most site wells sampled in 2017–2018 met Health Canada's drinking-water guidelines, except wells directly adjacent to the aeration stabilization basin with exceedances of aluminum, arsenic, cobalt, lead and/or chloride; manganese exceeded guidelines in several wells. The proponent attributed manganese to naturally elevated Nova Scotia background.

Health Canada, Nova Scotia Environment and Climate Change, and PLFN did not agree with excluding potable groundwater as an exposure pathway merely because there were no current potable wells on the site. Provincial reviewers described the PLFN wellfield as not fully confined because of fracture flow and recommended treating it as semi-confined or “leaky”. The provincial reviewer also said that this did not itself prove increased risk to the wellfield, but that uncertainty required a robust long-term groundwater monitoring programme.

**Independent monitoring implications:**
- Obtain the full well inventory, construction logs, surveyed elevations, screen intervals, hydraulic testing, water-level time series and every laboratory result, not only summary tables.
- Separate background manganese from potential site-related contributions using upgradient, cross-gradient and downgradient wells, lithology, redox, pH, dissolved/total fractions and time-series behaviour.
- Include PLFN wellfield sentinel locations only with PLFN direction and appropriate access/governance.
- Monitor both groundwater quality and quantity; include seasonal and tidal influence where hydrogeologically relevant.
- Verify that final provincial approvals and the final federal conditions actually require the proposed monitoring.

IAAC report passages: https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/evaluations/proj/80164/document/161580 (sections 5.3.1 and 5.3.2; groundwater discussion around paragraphs/pages corresponding to lines 947–954 in the HTML version).

### 2.2 Country foods and Strait-wide sediment transport

IAAC records Health Canada's concern that the proponent's HHERA screened out fish, shellfish, aquatic game, terrestrial game and other food pathways using methods Health Canada considered inadequate. Health Canada also said that bioaccumulation and biomagnification were not adequately considered. It recommended revisiting shellfish and other excluded pathways, designing monitoring with PLFN/Mi’kmaq input, adapting to new species that recolonize after closure, and extending post-closure monitoring beyond the estuary because suspended sediment could carry contaminants into the Northumberland Strait.

This is a direct rationale for the user's full-area scope. The independent programme must not end at the Boat Harbour outlet or a small near-field circle. It needs a connected estuary–Pictou Harbour–Northumberland Strait design with reference stations, gradients, sediment deposition zones and appropriate species/trophic levels. Exact stations must be set only after reviewing current bathymetry, hydrodynamics, historical coordinates, source datasets and PLFN priorities.

### 2.3 Residual hot spots and delineation uncertainty

IAAC records Health Canada's concern that using exposure-point concentrations to define the area requiring ex-situ sediment remediation could leave hot spots after remediation. It also notes that contaminated sediment may enter the Northumberland Strait after dam removal. The proponent was to develop a sediment sampling procedure under the provincial regulatory process, applied throughout remediation, to check that residual contamination is below remedial objectives before moving to a new area.

Independent monitoring must therefore include:
- independent split samples and blind duplicates;
- pre-dredge, during-dredge and post-dredge sediment grids/cores;
- vertical profiling, not just surface grabs;
- a transparent hot-spot decision rule;
- laboratory confirmation before an area is declared complete;
- suspended particulate and deposition monitoring outside the immediate estuary;
- public reporting of failed criteria, rework and residual areas.

### 2.4 Independent monitor and Indigenous monitors

The IAAC report states that a third-party independent environmental monitor is to be retained before site preparation and continue from site preparation through post-closure. The monitor is to observe and report on implementation of mitigation and follow-up programmes, with reports to the proponent, PLFN/Mi’kmaq of Nova Scotia and IAAC. It also requires Indigenous monitors, with a collaborative process to define their scope, purpose, objectives, locations, frequency, timing, duration, training, equipment, access and reporting.

The independent monitor requirement is not the same thing as an independently controlled environmental investigation. The final design should distinguish:
- **compliance observation:** whether the proponent performs required controls and monitoring;
- **independent verification:** separate sample collection, split samples, laboratory contracts, QA/QC, data custody and analysis;
- **rights-holder monitoring:** PLFN-defined Indigenous monitoring, governed by PLFN priorities and protocols.

The final programme must not assume that the independent monitor can override PLFN governance or publish sensitive knowledge.

### 3.1 Comment 59 — DFO and regional receiving-environment baseline gap

The 25 October 2024 comment submitted by W. A. Mac Quarrie includes DFO's 6 November 2024 technical comments. DFO stated that important baseline data gaps remained for the estuary and the marine environment labelled “Pictou Road” and that those gaps needed to be filled before an adequate effects assessment could be conducted. DFO also described the proposed Geotube process and questioned the evidence base around the marine environment.

The same comment raises a potentially important receiving-water confounder: the proximity of the PLFN sewage treatment plant and outfall, approximately one kilometre west of the Boat Harbour entrance, and whether its efficiency and discharge composition had been considered in post-remediation water-quality predictions. This is a question raised by the commenter, not proof that the outfall is causing contamination. It is a clear monitoring-design issue: map and characterize all municipal/community outfalls and other regional sources and include appropriate reference stations and source fingerprints.

### 3.2 Comment 48 — Health Canada technical review of Round 1 responses

The registry page describes Health Canada's review of proponent responses to IRs IAAC-33, 36, 37, 39 and 62c. Its summary flags:
- insufficient rationale for soil allocation factors used in site-specific target levels for vanadium and dioxins/furans;
- insufficient rationale to exclude human consumption of terrestrial game mammals as an operable exposure pathway;
- insufficient information about plant/berry tissue sampling.

The attachment is not fully analysed here. The summary is enough to establish a material follow-up lead, not to conclude that the issues remained unresolved in the final assessment.

### 3.3 Comment 47 — Health Canada review of a larger set of Round 1 responses

The registry describes Health Canada's review of responses to IAAC-32, 34, 35, 38, 40–61, 62a/b, 63, 64 and 65. Its summary identifies concerns including insufficient evaluation of health impacts associated with sediment release into the Northumberland Strait and sediment resuspension in the Boat Harbour Effluent Treatment Facility area during post-remediation. The complete attachment needs to be read and reconciled against final IAAC conditions and the final country-food follow-up programme.

### 3.4 Comment 50 — Nova Scotia groundwater and containment-cell model concerns

The provincial technical review includes a concern that manganese was described as naturally elevated without defining background concentrations or fully evaluating it. The reviewer questioned whether a groundwater sampling-results figure had been provided. It also questioned the unusually high porosity values used in the HELP model for the containment cell. This is a model-input verification issue: recover the original HELP model, parameter file, material properties, sensitivity runs and independent review response.

### 3.5 Comment 49 and comment 52 — PLFN technical review and final position

The registry identifies:
- PLFN's technical review of the EIS by EXP Services Inc. (15-page review, January 2021);
- PLFN's Final Position on the Proposed Boat Harbour Remediation Project (23 April 2024), with a separate PLFN response to the IR82 technical/economic assessment.

These are central rights-holder records. Their technical questions should be tracked in a response matrix, but the documents and any embedded Indigenous knowledge must be handled under PLFN's governance and confidentiality requirements. The repository should not reproduce sensitive content or treat it as open data simply because a document is in a public registry.

### 3.6 Comment 63 — DFO review of the draft EA report

DFO's 6 November 2024 submission is listed as comment 63. The first-page text confirms DFO's review of the draft report and proposed conditions. It should be reviewed in full for marine baseline gaps, sediment transport, fish habitat, dredging, leachate return and monitoring requirements, then compared with the final decision.

### 3.7 Comment 64 — Health Canada review of draft EA report and conditions

Health Canada said that some findings were appropriately reflected but identified remaining issues in the draft:
- daily exposure limits were insufficiently reflected;
- dust suppression mitigation was not appropriately reflected in the draft federal condition.

This submission is a useful draft-to-final audit item. The final condition wording and subsequent implementation plans must be checked; do not assume the draft comments automatically carried through unchanged.

### 3.8 Comment 65 — ECCC draft-condition corrections

ECCC recommended stronger controls for ground- and burrow-nesting birds, stockpile/bare-soil covering during nesting season and corrections to species-at-risk references. These are not core water/sediment chemistry issues, but they are relevant to a complete environmental monitoring programme and illustrate why the final conditions need a condition-by-condition compliance matrix.

### 3.9 Comment 68 — biological dredging proposal

Buckman Laboratories proposed biological dredging/bioaugmentation, with attachments describing a programme at other industrial lagoons. The registry attachment claims reductions in sludge volume and includes a reported four-month trial summary from a different facility. It is a vendor proposal and case-study claim, not Boat Harbour evidence. Before it could be considered, an independent technical review would need to establish:
- whether the relevant Boat Harbour sediment fraction is biodegradable organic matter or mineral/metal/organic-contaminant-bearing solids;
- whether the treatment mobilizes metals, methylmercury, PAHs, dioxins/furans or other contaminants;
- what happens to contaminants in residual solids, dissolved phase, gases and biomass;
- whether treatment changes leachability, toxicity or bioavailability;
- whether the claimed mass/volume reduction is supported by independent measurements and a complete mass balance;
- whether the process is compatible with the intended dredging, dewatering, containment and discharge approvals.

Biological treatment cannot destroy elemental metals. It can alter chemical form, mobility or partitioning, or transfer metals to residual material. The vendor's claims must not be adopted as a fact or recommendation without independent Boat Harbour-specific testing.

### 3.10 Comment 70 — Indigenous Services Canada

The ISC submission is listed as comment 70. It reviews the draft EA report and draft potential conditions, including concerns about the treatment of PLFN's alternative containment-cell proposal. It should be included in the condition-response matrix.

### 3.11 Comment 62 — PLFN draft report and conditions submission

PLFN submitted comments on the draft EA report and conditions on 14 November 2024. The record should be reviewed as a rights-holder submission and reconciled against the final decision and the judicial-review record. Do not extract or republish sensitive material outside PLFN's direction.

## 8. Bottom line

The most important newly confirmed issue is not a new contaminant detection. It is the combination of (a) a project still in pre-construction/approval uncertainty in the latest located status material, (b) an unfinished alternative-containment-cell process, (c) significant baseline and pathway concerns raised by DFO, Health Canada and provincial reviewers, and (d) the need to distinguish a compliance monitor from an independently controlled environmental investigation.

The independent monitoring project should therefore proceed as an evidence-recovery and governance programme first, followed by a refreshed, independently designed baseline and then construction/post-closure monitoring. It should not be represented as a ready-to-deploy drone chemical-detection system: laboratory chemistry remains essential, and drones provide reconnaissance/optical proxies only.
