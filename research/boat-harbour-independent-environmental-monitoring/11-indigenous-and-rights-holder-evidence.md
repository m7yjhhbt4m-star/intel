## 1. Why this is a separate evidence stream

The environmental record cannot be understood by treating Indigenous knowledge and rights-holder submissions as merely another stakeholder-comment category. Boat Harbour/A’se’k is a place with a specific history, relationship to PLFN, and consequences for land, water, food, health, culture, identity, and community well-being.

This document separates four things that are often collapsed:

1. **Rights-holder positions:** what PLFN or another Mi’kmaq government/community has itself stated.
2. **Indigenous knowledge studies:** studies documenting traditional use, ecological knowledge, and community-defined well-being, with their own authorship, methods, permissions, and limits.
3. **Government interpretation:** what IAAC, federal departments, or provincial officials say they understood from Indigenous submissions.
4. **Proponent/consultant material:** what Build Nova Scotia and its consultants report about Indigenous engagement, impacts, mitigation, or expected future use.

A government summary is evidence of the government's interpretation. It is not a replacement for the original rights-holder record. Likewise, one community's submission must not be represented as the position of every Mi’kmaq community.

## 3. PLFN's own stated position — first-pass findings

### 3.1 Final Position (23 April 2024)

The IAAC registry identifies comment 52 as **“Final Position of Pictou Landing First Nation on Proposed Boat Harbour Remediation Project,”** submitted by an administrator on behalf of PLFN on 23 April 2024. The registry lists two attachments: a document brief and the final-position PDF.

The indexed contents show that the submission addresses, among other matters:

- Aboriginal title;
- the existing landfill/containment cell and its asserted infringement of Aboriginal rights;
- the absence of a remediation agreement and asserted implications under section 35;
- consultation to date;
- proposed accommodation/compensation;
- other available lands; and
- PLFN's assessment that the Boat Harbour landfill is the worst option based on environmental criteria.

The same registry entry includes an attached PLFN document brief responding to Information Requirement 82 and the province's technical/economic feasibility assessment of an alternative containment-cell location. The brief is dated 1 August 2023. It is important to keep this PLFN response distinct from the province's own IR82 response and technical/economic assessment.

**Evidence status:** Registry metadata and the indexed table of contents have been verified. The complete attachment has not yet been retrieved and independently read page by page in this research pass. Therefore, the headings above are a reliable description of the document's stated topics, not a complete account of its legal argument or technical evidence.

### 3.2 What IAAC says about the PLFN position — and why this is a different source

The January 2025 IAAC Environmental Assessment Report states that PLFN's principal concerns included hazardous-waste storage on site, leakage back into Boat Harbour, and potential effects to drinking water. It also records PLFN's opposition to indefinite use/vertical expansion of the existing containment cell and describes PLFN's concern that this would perpetuate historical trauma and prevent full spiritual and psychological healing.

IAAC reports that the Assembly of Nova Scotia Mi’kmaq Chiefs passed a resolution on 25 April 2019, supported by all 13 Mi’kmaq communities in Nova Scotia, authorizing PLFN to lead consultation for the project on their behalf. This is an important governance fact about the consultation process. It does not mean every community necessarily holds identical views on every technical or political issue, and it does not remove the need to attribute each later submission to its actual author.

The IAAC report also distinguishes the proponent's prediction of positive environmental effects from PLFN's concern about permanent waste storage. It records that the proponent expected remediation to support future traditional use, while PLFN questioned whether traditional and cultural use could resume if the containment cell remained permanently in place.

**Interpretation rule:** When reporting these matters, cite the PLFN submission for PLFN's own position and cite IAAC separately for the government's account of that position. Do not cite IAAC as though it were the author of PLFN's view.

**Government source:** [IAAC Environmental Assessment Report, January 2025](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/evaluations/proj/80164/document/161580)

## 4. PLFN Well-Being Baseline Study — community evidence, not a clinical diagnosis

The EIS registry lists Appendix S as the *Pictou Landing First Nation Well-Being Baseline Study*. Its executive summary says that PLFN undertook the study from 10–18 November 2019 using a quantitative survey, qualitative focus groups and interviews, guided by the Mi’kmaw Logic Model for Indicators of Mi’kmaw Well-being. The summary describes the model as grounded in a holistic and relational worldview and as enabling Mi’kmaq to define their own vision of a healthy and thriving population.

The IAAC 2025 report draws on this study and reports several findings, including:

- nearly half of survey participants reported having run out of food for their family, and access to country foods would help address food insecurity;
- over two-thirds of respondents looked forward to A'se'k returning to how it once was, while fewer than one-quarter believed the project could accomplish this;
- two-thirds answered “no” or “don't know” when asked whether hunting, fishing, trapping and gathering could be restored around Boat Harbour; and
- participants described distrust, emotional and mental-health effects, and concern that a permanent containment cell would prevent the area from returning to its former state.

These figures are reported here as findings quoted or summarized by IAAC from the PLFN study. Before using them as independent quantitative estimates, the full Appendix S must be reviewed for sample size, recruitment, question wording, response denominators, missing responses, analysis methods and limitations. The study is community well-being evidence. It is not, on the material reviewed so far, a clinical study establishing that Boat Harbour contamination caused particular cancers, autoimmune diseases or other diagnoses.

**Monitoring implication:** An independent programme should not reduce “recovery” to chemical concentrations alone. With PLFN direction, it should consider community-defined indicators of access, trust, food security, ability to harvest, cultural practice, knowledge transfer, perceived safety and the quality of experience. Such indicators must be governed and interpreted by PLFN; they must not be imposed by an outside technical team.

**Source index:** [EIS Appendix S — Well-Being Baseline Study](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/evaluations/proj/80164/document/137959), listed as Appendix S.  
**Supporting summary:** [Study executive summary](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/137890E.pdf).

## 5. Mi’kmaq Ecological Knowledge Study — scope and governance

The EIS registry lists Appendix T as the *Mi’kmaq of Nova Scotia Ecological Knowledge Study*. The study's executive summary identifies Membertou Geomatics Solutions (MGS) as its developer, working for GHD Limited in relation to the proposed Boat Harbour Remediation Project. It says the mandate was to identify past and current Mi’kmaq traditional-use activities and existing Mi’kmaq ecological knowledge in the project lands and waters.

The summary describes two components:

- traditional land and resource use activities, past and present; and
- a Mi’kmaq significance-species analysis.

It says interviews were the key source for the traditional-use component and that the study followed the Assembly of Nova Scotia Mi’kmaq Chiefs' *Mi’kmaq Ecological Knowledge Protocol, 2nd Edition*.

**Authorship distinction:** Appendix T is a study developed by MGS for GHD, not a PLFN-authored final-position submission. It may contain knowledge obtained from Mi’kmaq interviewees and must be treated as Indigenous knowledge with its own confidentiality and governance requirements. Its inclusion in a proponent's EIS does not establish that every underlying interview, location, species record or cultural detail is open for unrestricted republication or mapping.

**Governance rule for this project:** Do not copy sensitive harvesting locations, culturally sensitive places, interview quotations, burial locations, medicinal knowledge or other restricted knowledge into this public-facing repository unless the appropriate knowledge holders/rightsholder authority has explicitly authorized that use. Record the existence, authorship, date, scope, protocol and access restrictions without reproducing protected content.

## 7. What this means for independent environmental monitoring

The rights-holder record changes what a technically adequate monitoring programme must be capable of answering. It does not predetermine the scientific results.

### 7.1 Questions the programme must be able to answer

Subject to PLFN's direction and research governance, the programme should be designed to test:

- whether groundwater pathways from the site, containment cell, leachate systems or connected water bodies could affect PLFN's wellfield or other receptors;
- whether surface-water, sediment and suspended-particulate transport moves contaminants from the remediation footprint into wetlands, the estuary, Pictou Harbour and the Northumberland Strait;
- whether contaminants enter or persist in fish, shellfish, plants, wildlife and other country-food species, using species and locations selected with the rights-holder;
- whether remediation, dredging, dewatering, temporary waste handling, cell operation and closure create short-term or long-term exposure pathways;
- whether the monitoring design is capable of detecting failures, not merely demonstrating compliance at a small set of proponent-selected locations; and
- whether the area is becoming usable and safe in ways that matter to PLFN, including cultural, spiritual, subsistence and intergenerational use.

### 7.2 Independence and governance requirements

An independent monitor is not independent merely because a consultant is labelled “independent.” The governance design should establish:

- who selects and can remove the independent monitor;
- who controls the sampling plan, station locations, analyte list and changes to them;
- whether PLFN can collect split samples and commission parallel laboratory analysis;
- who owns raw data, field notes, photographs, GPS coordinates and laboratory certificates;
- whether PLFN receives results at the same time as regulators and the proponent;
- who decides what data may be published, mapped or shared;
- how Indigenous knowledge and sensitive cultural information are protected;
- how disagreements, anomalous results and exceedances are escalated;
- whether monitoring continues through construction, operations, closure and post-closure, with enforceable triggers; and
- how community-defined well-being and traditional-use indicators are governed separately from laboratory data.

These are proposed design requirements, not a claim that the current decision statement already guarantees all of them.

## 11. Further findings from the IAAC report cross-check — 23 November 2026

The January 2025 IAAC report provides additional detail that materially sharpens the rights-holder evidence map. These are **government-recorded summaries or quotations of PLFN's position**, not a substitute for the underlying PLFN documents.

### 11.1 PLFN referenda on long-term containment-cell use

IAAC records that PLFN's April 2024 Final Position stated that PLFN held two referenda, in 2021 and 2023, on the use of Boat Harbour, and that in both cases 98% of those who voted opposed long-term use of the containment cell.

**What is established:** IAAC's final report records this as a statement made in PLFN's April 2024 submission.

**What is not yet independently established in this research:** the referendum questions, dates, eligible-voter counts, turnout, voting procedures, detailed results, and whether official referendum records are publicly available. The 98% figure must therefore be attributed as “PLFN reported, as recorded by IAAC,” until the original referendum records or full PLFN submission are reviewed.

### 11.2 The proponent-funded land-use plan was not an adopted PLFN plan

IAAC's report records a disagreement between the proponent and PLFN about the status of a future land-use plan. The proponent said it had funded development of a plan laying out a vision for Boat Harbour after remediation. IAAC records PLFN's statement that the plan had not been ratified by PLFN membership or approved through a formal Band Council Resolution of Chief and Council and was not an official PLFN plan.

**Implication:** The proponent-funded plan must not be represented as an adopted PLFN land-use decision, a community mandate, or consent to the proposed project. Obtain the plan, its authorship and engagement record, and any later PLFN resolution or formal adoption record before relying on it.

### 11.3 The independent environmental monitor — report recipient is not the same as governance control

In its assessment analysis, IAAC identifies a third-party independent environmental monitor to observe and report on implementation of mitigation and follow-up programmes from site preparation through post-closure. The report describes reporting to the proponent, PLFN, the Mi’kmaq of Nova Scotia and IAAC, with frequency and format to be determined in consultation with PLFN and IAAC.

This is relevant to the monitoring design, but it does not by itself answer the governance questions in Section 7.2. In particular, being a report recipient does not necessarily establish that PLFN selects the monitor, controls the scope, can require additional sampling, owns the raw data, or controls publication of sensitive knowledge. Those issues must be checked against the final Decision Statement, approved follow-up plans, provincial approvals and any agreement with PLFN.

### 11.4 Consultation representation is not a single undifferentiated mandate

IAAC's report says the Assembly of Mi’kmaq Chiefs passed a resolution on 25 April 2019, supported by all 13 Mi’kmaq communities in Nova Scotia, authorizing PLFN to lead consultation for this project on their behalf. The report's footnote also says Membertou, Millbrook and Sipekne’katik were, at the time described, consulted independently and were not represented by the Kwilmu’kw Maw-Klusuaqn Negotiation Office (KMKNO).

This reinforces the need to record the actual author and consultation route for each submission. The Assembly resolution is a project-specific consultation governance fact; it should not be simplified into a claim that every community's position on every later technical issue was identical. Sipekne’katik's own public-comment support for PLFN's Final Position is a separate, attributable record.

### 11.5 Health and monitoring caveat in IAAC's own assessment

The IAAC report states that it considered the proponent's proposed remedial objectives and recognized uncertainty about whether they would be fully protective of human health. It says that using the proponent's proposed objectives could create a greater potential for post-remediation contamination levels to exceed levels fully protective of human health compared with objectives that consider all operable exposure pathways, contaminants of potential concern and accurate site-exposure assumptions. IAAC also notes that residual contamination may remain in wetlands after remediation and that post-closure monitoring may trigger an updated human-health risk assessment and further mitigation, potentially including access restrictions or country-food bans.

This is a government assessment finding, not a PLFN statement. It matters to rights-holder concerns because a programme that relies on post-remediation restrictions could leave continued limits on access and harvesting even after the remediation works are declared complete.

### 8.3 Original community-based oral history: Castleden et al. (2017)

The full text of BH-127, Castleden et al. (2017), *“Put It Near the Indians”: Indigenous Perspectives on Pulp Mill Contaminants in Their Traditional Territories*, has now been reviewed. The paper was co-produced with the Pictou Landing Native Women's Group and reports conversational oral-history interviews with 10 PLFN Elders (eight women). It organizes the stories into four layers: A'se'k as an all-season, all-purpose place; A'se'k as a refuge; changes after the mill; and what was lost, gone or changed.

The Elders describe food harvesting, fish, shellfish, hunting, trapping, berries, medicines, swimming, skating, family gatherings, sharing and intergenerational teaching before the mill. They also describe fish kills, loss of wildlife/harvesting, odours, changes to homes and diet, fear about health and loss of trust after industrial contamination. These are participants' lived experiences and observations; they are not a contaminant measurement or epidemiological proof of a specific disease cause.

The paper states that participants reviewed transcripts and that preliminary findings were vetted with participants and the PLFN Native Women's Group. The group requested a digital archive under OCAP® principles; recorded interviews remain in the hands of the PLFN Native Women's Group. The authors describe the work as one contribution to an ongoing dialogue, not a complete account of every Elder's relationship with A'se'k.

### 8.4 Documentary and research-governance requirements

- Use the published paper as a public source for the themes and methods; do not treat it as permission to access or republish the underlying audio/video archive.
- The paper's use of real names reflects participant choice in that research project; it does not automatically authorize reuse of names, images, quotations or recordings in a new documentary.
- Seek direction and permission from PLFN and the Native Women's Group before requesting or using raw interviews, unpublished transcripts, maps of harvesting places or other community knowledge.
- Keep participant testimony distinct from measured environmental results and from legal claims in the 2023–2024 PLFN submissions.
- Do not infer that all PLFN members share the same experience or position from a 10-Elder oral-history study or from a legal submission.
- Trace the legal brief's oral-history excerpts back to Castleden et al. (2017) and, where appropriate, to the community-controlled original archive with PLFN direction.



## 9. Community-led cancer-registry research and the JEHMC — Lewis et al. (2020)

The full text of Lewis et al. (2020), *Governmental Fiduciary Failure in Indigenous Environmental Health Justice: The Case of Pictou Landing First Nation* (BH-128), has been reviewed. The paper is co-authored by PLFN community researchers and describes a community-driven effort to access 2013 Canadian Cancer Registry microdata through the Atlantic Research Data Centre. The authors identified 55 cancer diagnoses among people residing on PLFN reserve subdivisions at diagnosis during 1992–2013 and compared the distribution of broad cancer categories with other populations.

The paper reports higher proportions of digestive, respiratory, male genital and urinary cancer diagnoses and a lower proportion of breast/female genital diagnoses. The published table reports proportions of diagnoses, not population-based age-standardized incidence rates; it does not provide person-time denominators, confidence intervals or adjusted risk estimates. It excludes members living off-reserve at diagnosis and does not link individual environmental exposures to cases. The study is therefore an important community-led disparity signal and a critique of the failure to answer health concerns, not proof that Boat Harbour or mill emissions caused cancer. The authors call for more in-depth and updated studies.

The paper describes the Joint Environmental Health Monitoring Committee (JEHMC), its 1990s mandate, the 1997 CanTox assessment, the 2008 Dillon review of 106 studies and the 2015 JEHMC report. **JEHMC is distinct from BHEAC**; they were established in different periods for different purposes and must not be conflated. The paper's statement about the 1995 settlement Act and Canadian liability should be checked against the actual Act and settlement agreement before legal conclusions are drawn.

Any future health research must be community-led, respect PLFN governance and privacy, and use appropriate Indigenous data sovereignty protocols. Do not seek or publish identifiable health records outside authorized, privacy-protected processes.



## 10. 2014 PLFN Environmental Health Survey — community-defined health measures

The full text of Lewis et al. (2021), *If Only They Had Accessed the Data* (BH-129), has been reviewed. The paper reports the 2014 Environmental Health Survey developed with the Pictou Landing Native Women's Group, using a Mi'kmaw environmental-health framework and Etuaptmumk/Two-Eyed Seeing. Thirteen community research assistants were hired; 279 people participated (59% response rate based on a population of 470); the 297-question survey generated over 400 variables.

The published paper presents selected measures of physical, mental, emotional and spiritual health and a community-defined environmental-dispossession question about whether participants feel the air, land and water around them will hurt them. It reports associations between environmental fear and self-rated health/depression and between racism and several health dimensions. These are self-reported cross-sectional associations, not proof of a specific chemical exposure or disease cause. The paper's bivariate analysis and comparisons with CCHS/RHS populations have limitations; the 2014 EHS is not a pre-exposure baseline.

This is important because it demonstrates how PLFN defined environmental health beyond conventional toxicology. Future monitoring and health assessment should be co-designed with PLFN and include community-defined indicators of relationships to land, water, food, medicine, culture and spiritual well-being. Keep these indicators distinct from laboratory measurements and clinical outcomes.

The paper presents only selected variables from the full survey; supplementary data/questionnaire and any later community health survey should be sought only with PLFN/NWG authorization. Raw individual-level data remain subject to community governance and privacy.
