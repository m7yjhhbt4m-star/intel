# Remediation Process, Geotube Dewatering and Leachate Monitoring

**Version:** 1.0 — 21 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Examine the proposed dredging/dewatering process and identify independent monitoring points that could be overlooked if the investigation focuses only on the lagoon and Strait.

## 1. Why this workstream matters

The proposed remediation does not simply excavate sediment and remove it. The project concept includes wet dredging, slurry pumping, Geotube dewatering, treatment of dewatering water/contact water, temporary storage and placement in an expanded containment cell. Each step creates potential pathways for particulate and dissolved contaminants to move between sediment, water, geotextile filtrate, leachate, surface drainage, groundwater and receiving environments.

An independent programme should monitor the entire material and water balance, not only ambient water at the lagoon edge.

## 2. Source-backed design context

### 2.1 GHD Design Requirements Document (2017)

The 2017 Design Requirements Document describes:
- Boat Harbour as a 142-hectare lagoon formerly connected to Pictou Harbour and the Northumberland Strait.
- The treatment sequence through twin settling basins, an aeration-stabilization basin and the stabilization lagoon.
- Sludge generally described as 10–20 cm thick, with some areas in excess of 1 m. This is an early design-document description, not a complete measured thickness map.
- Metals, PAHs and dioxins/furans in sludge; VOCs, petroleum hydrocarbons and chlorophenols were described as potential contaminants expected to be present.
- Surface water and groundwater as media requiring consideration in remediation.

Source: [GHD Design Requirements Document, September 2017](https://boatharbourproject.buildns.ca/s/Design-Requirements-Document.pdf).

### 2.2 Laboratory treatability study

The project resource page lists a Laboratory Treatability Study. Its stated test programme included:
- wet sediment removal;
- Geotube dewatering;
- treatment requirements for dewatering water;
- leach testing of dewatered sediment;
- stabilization of non-dewatered sediment;
- dry excavation scenarios and treatment of pumped surface water;
- a “do nothing” scenario with leach testing of untreated sediment.

Source: [Laboratory Treatability Study PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5fa9ac321d1bf94e1f13e118/t/5fb2fbd5cce468531e584c73/1605565406097/Laboratory+Treatability+Study.pdf).

### 2.3 Pilot-scale testing

The Environmental Impact Statement describes a pilot-scale programme completed between August 2018 and July 2019. It included isolation berms, aqua dams, silt curtains, bulk dewatering, mechanical and hydraulic dredging, Geotube dewatering, sludge management and optimization of water-treatment systems.

The EIS states that:
- sludge/sediment could be hydraulically dredged;
- enhanced grade controls were needed to minimize generation of additional waste;
- dry dredging was not suitable for Boat Harbour or the estuary because the underlying marine sediments were too soft to support construction equipment;
- dry removal could be suitable for settling basins and likely the aeration stabilization basin;
- Geotube dewatering was successful but took longer than anticipated, according to the project's public pilot summary;
- freezing weather stopped the pilot work earlier than planned.

Sources:
- [Environmental Impact Statement](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/137220E.pdf)
- [Pilot Scale Testing Report entry](https://boatharbourproject.buildns.ca/resources) (linked to Google Drive by the proponent)
- [Project pilot testing summary](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/137875E.pdf)

### 2.4 Water fraction in dewatering effluent

A 2022 proponent response to information requests states that pilot dewatering effluent was a combination of Boat Harbour stabilization-lagoon water, porewater in the dredged sediment and water retained in the sediment. It states that the in-place sludge was approximately 9–14% solids and the pumped slurry approximately 1.5–7.5% solids, and that more than 60% of dewatering effluent was represented by lagoon water quality at the time of dredging.

This matters because effluent quality cannot be predicted solely from bulk sediment chemistry. It depends on lagoon water, porewater, slurry dilution, solids content, polymer/coagulant use, pH, redox and filtration performance.

Source: [IAAC Round 1 Information Request Response, document 144780E.pdf](https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/144780E.pdf).

## 3. Geotextile filtration and metal migration — Tackley et al. (2021)

**Paper:** Tackley, H.A., Lake, C.B. & Alimohammadi, M. (2021). “Examining metal migration through geotextiles during dewatering.” *Geotextiles and Geomembranes*, 49(1), 32–40. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geotexmem.2020.09.002.

This study used contaminated sediment from Boat Harbour and examined Cu, Pb and Zn mobility during geotextile filtration. It compared bench-scale testing and a field test. Two chemical additives (cationic coagulant and cationic polymer) were used to improve filtration. The authors measured total and dissolved metals and particulate matter in filtrate and examined pH, oxidation-reduction potential and zeta potential.

Main findings from the article abstract/highlights:
- As a filter cake built up on the geotextile, filtrate quality improved for the metals and particulate matter examined.
- Metals detected in the effluent were primarily in the solid/particulate state.
- The authors concluded that further reduction might be possible through subsequent filtration.
- The paper notes gaps in understanding the mechanisms of metal transport through geotextiles.

**Important limit:** This is a study of three metals (Cu, Pb, Zn) and a particular sediment/geotextile/conditioning configuration. It does not prove that all contaminants, all dissolved species or all full-scale Geotube filtrate will behave the same way. It is not a complete performance validation for the proposed full-scale treatment system.

Source: [Tackley et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geotexmem.2020.09.002).

## 4. Independent monitoring requirements for the dewatering train

### 4.1 Sample every material/water stream

Define monitoring points before work starts:
1. lagoon source water before mixing;
2. dredged slurry before polymer/coagulant addition;
3. conditioned slurry immediately before Geotube feed;
4. Geotube filtrate at early, middle and mature filter-cake stages;
5. any secondary filtration or treatment stage influent/effluent;
6. temporary leachate treatment facility influent and effluent;
7. contact water and stormwater collection;
8. containment-cell leachate collection;
9. groundwater monitoring wells around the cell;
10. final discharge or off-site transfer point.

The specific locations and sampling frequencies must be agreed with PLFN and regulators and adapted to the final design. The list is a proposed independent observation framework, not an assertion that all these points are currently installed.

### 4.2 Analyze total and dissolved fractions

For metals, measure total and dissolved fractions separately where appropriate. Include TSS and particle-size distribution so that particulate transport is visible. A low dissolved concentration does not establish low total metal transport if particulate matter is passing through.

At minimum, the dewatering train should include:
- Cu, Pb, Zn (directly addressed in the Tackley study);
- As, Cd, Cr and Hg, based on the broader site sediment evidence;
- PCDD/PCDF and PAHs where source sediment and treatment pathway justify testing;
- TSS, pH, conductivity, salinity, alkalinity, DOC/TOC and redox-related parameters;
- selected VOCs, petroleum hydrocarbons, chlorophenols or other site-supported analytes where relevant to the source material.

### 4.3 QA/QC and operating variables

Record:
- Geotube fabric specification, lot and installation;
- slurry solids percentage and feed rate;
- polymer/coagulant identity and dose;
- pH, temperature, redox and conductivity;
- cumulative volume processed and filtrate flow;
- filter-cake thickness/age and pressure/head;
- maintenance, tears, bypasses and abnormal flow;
- weather and temperature, especially freezing conditions.

Use duplicate samples and field/lab blanks; retain raw laboratory certificates and reporting limits. Establish control limits and stop-work triggers before operation begins.

## 5. Containment cell and leachate pathway

The BGC External Technical Review identified uncertainties in:
- the condition of the existing berms and global slope stability;
- the ability to prepare the liner subgrade over residual sludge/wastewater;
- compaction and containment of end-dumped sludge;
- Geotube consolidation and differential settlement effects on the cover;
- the representativeness of the single underdrain sample used in some water-quality calculations;
- the adequacy of HELP modelling assumptions and leachate generation estimates;
- full-scale treatment performance and final discharge criteria;
- detailed sampling and QA/QC arrangements for different water and leachate streams.

These are review findings about design uncertainty, not proof of failure. They support a monitoring package with:
- groundwater levels and chemistry around the cell;
- leachate quantity and chemistry;
- surface-water monitoring at drainage pathways;
- cell settlement and deformation surveys;
- liner/berm construction QA records;
- transparent reporting of exceedances, corrective actions and trends.

## 6. Independent programme design principle

Do not treat the Geotube/containment system as an engineering black box. The contaminant pathway is a material balance:

**Source sediment + lagoon water → dredged slurry → conditioned slurry → Geotube filtrate + dewatered solids → treatment/discharge or containment.**

Every arrow is a potential transfer point. The independent monitor should verify both the solid residuals and all liquid/particulate outputs.

## 7. Numerical pilot-scale findings recovered through BGC review — 22 November 2026

The BGC 2021 External Technical Review quotes the GHD Pilot Scale Testing Construction Report (23 December 2019, 580 pages):

- Average slurry solids were 5.2% in Phase 1 and 2.4% in Phase 2; values as low as 1.0–1.5% occurred despite a 5% target.
- BGC noted that low solids could extend Geotube dewatering time and delay construction.
- Geotubes initially dewatered rapidly, but much of the volume reduction occurred over time. BGC cites one Phase 1 example with an overall 28% volume reduction after four months (net volume change of 63% when accounting for the reported bulking factor).
- The pilot results were considered promising but limited in sample count relative to the anticipated full-scale material volume approaching 1 million m³.
- BGC noted that final discharge criteria were still in development, leaving uncertainty about whether the full-scale temporary leachate treatment facility would meet final performance criteria.
- The water balance used HELP modelling, while site-specific leachate-quality modelling relied on a single underdrain liquid sample from MH-1.

These are scale-up and design-confidence limitations, not proof of failure. They strengthen the need to obtain the entire 580-page pilot report, all laboratory certificates and process logs, and to monitor the complete liquid and solids mass balance during any full-scale operation.

Source: [BGC Engineering, External Technical Review – Final, 29 January 2021](https://aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/141419E.pdf), especially the pilot-testing discussion and leachate modelling review.
