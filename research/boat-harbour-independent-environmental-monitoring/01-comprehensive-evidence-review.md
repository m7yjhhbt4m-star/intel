# Boat Harbour / A’se’k — Comprehensive Evidence Review

**Version:** 2.1 — expanded with registry completeness audit, 22 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Evidence review for an independent environmental investigation of Boat Harbour/A’se’k, the associated wetlands and waterways, the estuary, and the Northumberland Strait receiving environment.  
**Status:** Desk research. No new field samples have been collected. This is not a regulatory compliance determination, human-health risk assessment, legal opinion, or proof of contaminant migration outside the sampled areas.

## 1. Executive assessment

### 1.1 What the evidence establishes

1. **Boat Harbour is a documented legacy industrial-effluent site.** A natural tidal estuary connected to the Northumberland Strait was converted into a closed effluent stabilization basin in 1967. Pulp-mill effluent entered the treatment system from 1967 to January 2020. Effluent from the adjacent Canso Chemicals chlor-alkali facility was also treated there from 1971 to 1992. The federal assessment describes early raw-effluent routing through a natural wetland/settling area before the later settling-basin and aeration system was built.

2. **The lagoon sediment has a long record of metal contamination.** Hoffman et al. (2017) reviewed 103 Boat Harbour sediment samples from 81 stations and four reference locations, using secondary records collected from 1992–2015. Seven metal(loid)s (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Hg and Zn) exceeded at least one low-effect sediment guideline in some samples. Several exceeded probable-effect levels (PEL) in a substantial proportion of samples. The historical dataset was not spatially or vertically adequate to delineate all contamination.

3. **Organic contaminants are a separate and serious line of evidence.** Hoffman et al. (2019) reviewed 1992–2015 secondary data for PCDD/Fs and PAHs. PCDD/F toxic-equivalency concentrations exceeded the highest-effect thresholds in Boat Harbour sediment. Five individual PAH compounds frequently exceeded severe-effect thresholds, even though total PAHs were below the severe-effect threshold. The authors found important horizontal, vertical and temporal gaps and recommended detailed sampling in the lagoon and down-gradient receiving environments.

4. **Contamination is not confined to the main lagoon dataset.** Quanz et al. (2021) sampled sediment, surface water and dragonfly larvae from five site wetlands and two reference wetlands. Their study found site-specific exceedances for As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Zn, total mercury and PCDD/PCDF. The most striking result was a PCDD/PCDF TEQ sediment value of 594 ng TEQ/kg at a deep-water sampling point in WL2; the other two WL2 values were 2.0 and 2.6 ng TEQ/kg. The study also identified exceedances in WL5, which had not been included in the consultant-led risk assessment discussed by the authors.

5. **Ecological availability is not the same as total sediment concentration.** A 2020 DGT passive-sampling study deployed 80 devices at the sediment/water interface in Boat Harbour, a de-watered cove, surrounding wetlands and the Northumberland Strait. It reported low DGT-labile metal(loid) and total-Hg concentrations and MeHg below detection in the deployment period. DGT measures a labile/bioavailable fraction over a defined deployment, not total contaminant inventory, not all exposure routes, and not the chemistry of every sediment layer. It does not negate the bulk-sediment guideline exceedances.

6. **The marine receiving-environment baseline is limited in footprint and duration.** Chaudhary et al. (2020) sampled a defined set of stations along two near-field transects from the estuary mouth and a short coastal mussel line. Sediment concentrations of measured metals and dioxins/furans were generally below the cited sediment guidelines in the samples collected. However, the study itself followed a review that found historical marine-biota data too inconsistent to establish a robust baseline. The 2020 baseline is useful but cannot be extrapolated to the entire Northumberland Strait.

7. **A second lobster baseline (2018 sampling, published 2023) adds a wider-distance comparison.** Maltby et al. sampled three lobster age classes at sites 4, 20 and 70 km from the Boat Harbour estuary. Arsenic was elevated in all age classes and sites (4.8–12.68 mg/kg); mercury and methylmercury were approximately 0.04 mg/kg and minimally exceeded one guideline in some age-class/site combinations. There was no consistent pattern of accumulation by age class or site. Total arsenic is not equivalent to inorganic arsenic, and this result is not by itself a food-safety determination.

8. **The 2025 federal assessment identifies major sediment-mobilization and monitoring issues.** Proponent modelling predicted approximately 270,000 m³ of suspended solids could be mobilized after dam removal, with average daily TSS initially around ten times the applicable marine guideline threshold and falling below it within 140 days in the model. Modelled deposition in much of the embayment was 4–10 cm. These are predictions, not measured outcomes. Fisheries and Oceans Canada raised concerns about old 2003 marine sediment data, inadequate benthic habitat information, model-domain limitations and uncertainty about the sediment endpoint.

9. **The project had not begun implementation monitoring in the 2025 reporting year.** Build Nova Scotia’s 2025 annual report states that the project remained in pre-construction while the judicial review proceeded. It reports no project follow-up monitoring completed in that year and no specific implementation of the independent environmental monitor condition. “No non-compliance reported” in a year with no project monitoring is not evidence that environmental conditions were measured and found compliant.

### 1.2 What the evidence does not establish

- It does not establish that contaminants have spread throughout the Northumberland Strait.
- It does not establish that the wider Strait is free of contamination.
- It does not provide a current, complete, depth-resolved sediment inventory for the lagoon and all associated wetlands.
- It does not provide a complete seasonal water-quality baseline for the estuary and marine receiving environment.
- It does not allow drone imagery to identify dissolved mercury, methylmercury, arsenic, cadmium, dioxins/furans, PAHs or PFAS as individual chemicals.
- It does not show that all 25 wetlands, all waterways, or the marine regional study area have been sampled adequately.
- It does not provide the independent project follow-up monitoring results that would be expected after site preparation/remediation begins; the 2025 annual report says such monitoring had not yet occurred.

### 1.3 Overall conclusion

There is enough source-backed evidence to justify an independent monitoring programme. The defensible approach is to combine a historical-data/GIS reconstruction, water and sediment sampling, depth-resolved sediment cores, targeted biota monitoring, in-situ water-quality and passive-sampler measurements, bathymetry/sonar, and calibrated drone imagery. Laboratory chemistry must remain the basis for identifying and quantifying contaminants.

The programme must test for transport into the Strait rather than presume it. It must also avoid treating a small set of near-field marine stations as a survey of the whole receiving environment.

## 2. Site history and physical setting

### 2.1 Industrial timeline established in the federal assessment

- **1967:** The provincial government constructed the Boat Harbour Effluent Treatment Facility and converted the natural tidal estuary into a closed stabilization basin.
- **1967–1972:** Before construction of the later settling basins and aeration stabilization basin, raw effluent was discharged from a pipeline into an effluent ditch east of Highway 348, which flowed through a natural wetland area used as settling ponds before entering Boat Harbour.
- **1971–1992:** Effluent from Canso Chemicals Ltd., a chlor-alkali plant producing chlorine, caustic soda and hydrogen for the pulp mill, was treated in the facility.
- **1996:** A hazardous-waste containment cell was constructed and used for dewatered sludge from the aeration stabilization basin.
- **2014:** A pipeline break released more than 47 million litres of untreated pulp-and-paper effluent into an area leading to the East River/Pictou Harbour. Environment and Climate Change Canada reported the Fisheries Act offence and $225,000 penalty in 2016.
- **2015:** The Boat Harbour Act established the deadline for cessation of effluent receipt.
- **July 2019:** A pilot-scale testing programme was completed.
- **31 January 2020:** The treatment facility ceased receiving effluent under the Boat Harbour Act.

The 2014 pipeline release is a separate release pathway and should not be conflated with the historical lagoon sediment inventory. It warrants its own spatial evidence review: the pipeline route, spill area, wetland receiving area, East River pathway and Pictou Harbour receiving environment.

### 2.2 Site and study-area dimensions

The 2025 federal assessment describes the project Site Study Area (SSA) as approximately **546 hectares**. It includes the stabilization lagoon, settling basins, aeration stabilization basin, dam, effluent ditches, pipeline, estuary, wetlands, uplands and hazardous-waste containment cell.

Other dimensions reported in the assessment include:
- The former natural tidal estuary was about 142 hectares (Build Nova Scotia project description).
- The assessment identifies approximately 25 wetland areas covering about 86 hectares.
- The estuary/marsh complex is reported at approximately 10.02 hectares in the assessment.
- Nineteen watercourses were identified within the SSA; several were intermittent or dry at the time of assessment.

**Do not combine these areas as if they are mutually exclusive or use them to infer an exact contaminated footprint.** The 546-hectare SSA is a project-assessment boundary, not a measured contamination boundary.

### 2.3 Marine study boundaries — important distinction

The federal assessment's Appendix C defines the marine environment differently from most terrestrial components:
- Marine SSA: the estuary and Northumberland Strait shoreline immediately outside the estuary mouth, plus the portion of the East River containing the underwater pipeline.
- Marine LSA: Northumberland Strait waters within 500 m of the Pictou Road shoreline and waters within 500 m of the underwater pipeline in the East River.
- Marine RSA: the portion of the Northumberland Strait extending from the east side of the Confederation Bridge to the eastern coast of Prince Edward Island.

This is a broad regional assessment boundary. It is **not** evidence that this entire area was sampled at a density adequate for contaminant delineation. The 2020 marine baseline sampled a much smaller set of near-field transects and coastal mussel locations. Study boundary and sample footprint must be mapped separately.

## 3. Sediment metals — Hoffman et al. (2017)

**Paper:** Hoffman, E., Lyons, J., Boxall, J., Robertson, C., Lake, C.B. & Walker, T.R. (2017). “Spatiotemporal assessment (quarter century) of pulp mill metal(loid) contaminated sediment to inform remediation decisions.” *Environmental Monitoring and Assessment*, 189, 257. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-017-5952-0. Full text record: https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/items/3ef900df-0350-4b9a-b5ae-659b78c4f7fd

### 3.1 Study design and limitations

- 103 sediment samples from 81 Boat Harbour stations, plus four reference locations.
- Secondary data from 1992–2015, assembled from a review of more than 200 documents; eight reports contained relevant sediment metal chemistry data.
- About 69% of samples were shallow (0–15 cm).
- About 38% of the historic samples were grabs and 62% cores, according to later synthesis of the data.
- Methods varied across original studies; samples were not collected under one uniform, modern design.
- GIS review showed inadequate spatial coverage, especially in the western half compared with the eastern tidal channel.
- Core deformation and variable sample methods/depths could affect vertical interpretation.
- The paper recommends additional vertical and horizontal sampling and local background/reference concentrations.

### 3.3 Temporal and vertical interpretation

- Cd, Cu, Pb, Hg and Zn were significantly higher in 1998–2000 than in 1992–1996 and 2003–2015.
- The paper describes a decline after peak concentrations in 1998, except for Hg.
- The 2015 samples were below PELs, and many were at or below ISQGs. This does not demonstrate that all sediment in the lagoon had reached background conditions.
- Historical samples were heavily weighted toward the upper 15 cm, leaving deeper horizons poorly characterized.
- The authors noted that contaminated sediment may be under 30 cm at many stations based on earlier work, but this does not replace direct depth-resolved delineation at each depositional zone.

**Interpretation:** The dataset supports a historical metal-contamination finding within the facility. It does not provide a complete current inventory or a reliable map of all contaminant thicknesses. A new survey should retain discrete depth intervals and avoid homogenizing whole cores.

## 4. Organic sediment contaminants — Hoffman et al. (2019)

**Paper:** Hoffman, E., Alimohammadi, M., Lyons, J., Davis, E., Walker, T.R. & Lake, C.B. (2019). “Characterization and spatial distribution of organic-contaminated sediment derived from historical industrial effluents.” *Environmental Monitoring and Assessment*, 191, 590. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-019-7763-y. Open repository copy: https://dalspaceb.library.dal.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/646463e2-ae03-4297-9ac4-4a4fcbefba72/content

### 4.1 Findings

- Reviewed secondary monitoring data from 1992–2015 across Boat Harbour and off-site reference locations.
- Target groups included polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins and dibenzofurans (PCDD/Fs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).
- PCDD/F toxic equivalency (TEQ) concentrations exceeded the highest sediment-effect thresholds in Boat Harbour.
- Elevated PCDD/F concentrations persisted despite the 1992 pulp-and-paper dioxin/furan regulations.
- Five individual PAH compounds frequently exceeded severe-effect thresholds, even though total PAHs were below severe-effect thresholds.
- PAH diagnostic ratios suggested pyrogenic sources associated with wood processing or coal combustion. This is source interpretation, not proof of a single source or release event.
- The study identified inadequate horizontal, vertical and temporal coverage; deeper sediment horizons were less well characterized than 0–15 cm surface sediment.
- A later thesis synthesis reports PCDD/F TEQ calculations for 60 samples from 48 stations. The exact sample set and station-level values should be confirmed against the paper's supplementary material before station-level use.

### 4.2 Monitoring implications

- PCDD/Fs must remain in the sediment analytical programme; a metal-only programme is inadequate.
- Report individual congeners and TEQ, with the TEF scheme and detection limits documented.
- PAHs should be reported by individual compound and summed groups; total PAH alone can conceal individual compounds exceeding benchmarks.
- Grain size and total organic carbon should accompany sediment chemistry because organic matter and fine particles affect contaminant distribution and interpretation.
- The 2019 paper specifically recommends detailed sampling within Boat Harbour, local baseline concentrations and down-gradient receiving-environment sampling.

## 5. Wetlands — Quanz et al. (2021)

**Paper:** Quanz, M.E., Walker, T.R., Oakes, K. & Willis, R. (2021). “Contaminant characterization in wetland media surrounding a pulp mill industrial effluent treatment facility.” *Wetlands Ecology and Management*, 29, 209–229. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11273-020-09779-0. Open full text: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11273-020-09779-0

### 5.1 Design

- Seven wetlands were included: five site wetlands (WL1–WL5) and two reference wetlands (REF1, REF2).
- The project literature identified 25 wetland areas; this study did not sample all 25.
- Media: sediment, surface water and composite dragonfly larvae (Libellulidae) tissue.
- Sediment samples represented the 0–10 cm horizon. Three sediment samples were collected across shallow, medium and deep water-column locations in each wetland.
- Surface-water samples were collected before sediment sampling at corresponding locations.
- Sediment and surface-water metals were analyzed by ICP-MS; total mercury by an appropriate mercury-specific method; PCDD/PCDF by high-resolution mass spectrometry.
- The study analyzed metals, total Hg and PCDD/PCDF; it was not a full broad-spectrum organic contaminant survey.

### 5.2 Sediment findings

Mean sediment metal concentrations shown in the paper's figure (mg/kg; values are figure-derived and should be treated as approximate where graph extraction rather than a numerical table is used):

| Wetland | As | Cd | Cr | Cu | THg | Pb | Zn |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Reference (REF1) | 3.6 | 0.4 | 12 | 10 | 0.06 | 29 | 55 |
| WL1 | 8.0 | 2.4 | 41 | 31 | 0.17 | 28.5 | 220 |
| WL2 | 9.6 | 2.0 | 45 | 40 | 0.31 | 31.5 | 250 |
| WL3 | 7.0 | 0.2 | 6 | 5 | 0.03 | 9 | 35 |
| WL4 | 7.6 | 0.6 | 20 | 20 | 0.12 | 25 | 75 |
| WL5 | 10.0 | 6.3 | 36 | 69 | 0.40 | 59 | 705 |

Key comparisons reported by the authors:
- WL3 and WL4 exceeded the As ISQG (5.9 mg/kg).
- WL1 exceeded ISQGs for As, Cd, Cr and Zn.
- WL2 exceeded ISQGs for As, Cd, Cr, Cu and Zn.
- WL5 exceeded ISQGs for As, Cu and Pb and exceeded higher-tier values for Cd (3.5 mg/kg) and Zn (315 mg/kg).
- WL1, WL2 and WL5 exceeded the THg ISQG (0.17 mg/kg).
- Mean PCDD/PCDF TEQ in REF1, WL2 and WL4 exceeded the ISQG (0.85 ng TEQ/kg). WL2 exceeded the cited 21.5 ng TEQ/kg Tier 1/PEL value.
- One deep-water WL2 sediment sample had 594 ng TEQ/kg; two other WL2 TEQ values were 2.0 and 2.6 ng TEQ/kg. This is an extreme within-wetland spatial contrast and argues against relying on shallow shoreline-only sampling.

### 5.3 Surface-water findings

The study reported site-specific exceedances, including:
- Cadmium at WL3 and WL5 relative to the cited long-term guideline.
- Chromium at WL3, WL5 and REF1 relative to the conservative 1 µg/L comparison used in the paper.
- Copper and lead at WL3 and WL5 relative to conservative screening values (2 and 1 µg/L respectively).
- Total Hg at WL5: 0.064 µg/L compared with the cited long-term guideline of 0.026 µg/L.
- Zinc at WL3 and WL5: 96 and 138 µg/L, respectively, above the cited 30–37 µg/L comparison values.

The paper notes that water hardness was unknown, so conservative Cu and Pb guideline values were used. Surface-water concentrations vary with season, water chemistry and hydrology; a single sampling round cannot establish year-round conditions.

### 5.4 Biota and ecological interpretation

- Composite dragonfly larvae tissue samples were compared with reference tissue concentrations plus 20%; there are no directly applicable Odonata tissue guidelines in the study.
- Elevated As, Cu and Pb tissue concentrations occurred at two site wetlands, but reference THg and PCDD/PCDF tissue concentrations were higher than near-facility values.
- Insufficient tissue mass prevented stronger statistical treatment; Cd and Cr tissue values were below reporting limits.
- Authors interpreted ecological risk to macroinvertebrates from current effluent inputs as relatively low in the studied wetlands, while also identifying sediment and surface-water guideline exceedances.
- This is not a finding that all wetlands are uncontaminated or that all ecological pathways are low-risk.

### 5.5 Follow-up macroinvertebrate study

Quanz et al. (2021), “Effects of industrial effluent on wetland macroinvertebrate community structures near a wastewater treatment facility,” *Ecological Indicators*. The study sampled five site wetlands and two reference wetlands during spring, summer and fall 2018 using triplicate two-minute dip-net sweeps per wetland. It identified 31 families in spring, 33 in summer and 17 in fall. WL1/WL2 communities were not significantly different from reference sites in the main comparisons, while WL3 had a high proportion of pollution-tolerant groups in each season. Limitations included family-level identification, dip-net-only sampling, small sample sizes, differing wetland sizes and seasonal drying. Community similarity does not invalidate chemical exceedances; chemistry and biological community metrics answer different questions.

**Interpretation:** Wetlands require their own sampling design. Prioritize deeper depositional areas, historical discharge/settling areas, wetland WL5 and other unsampled wetlands, while retaining genuine reference wetlands. Do not infer from one wetland to all 25.

## 6. Bioavailable metals at the sediment/water interface — DGT study (2020)

**Paper:** Chaudhary, M., Quanz, M., Williams, J., Maltby, E., Oakes, K., Spooner, I. & Walker, T.R. (2020). “Assessment of metal(loid) concentrations using diffusive gradient thin (DGT) films in marine, freshwater and wetland aquatic ecosystems impacted by industrial effluents.” *Case Studies in Chemical and Environmental Engineering*, 2, 100041. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cscee.2020.100041. Open article: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666016420300396

### 6.1 Design and what DGT measures

- 80 DGT discs: 40 Chelex binding gels for As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb and Zn; 40 MFS binding gels for total Hg and methylmercury.
- Four deployment areas: water-covered Boat Harbour sediment, a de-watered cove, surrounding wetlands and Northumberland Strait marine sediment.
- Devices were positioned within 5 cm of the sediment/water interface.
- Marine deployment was one week (21–28 May 2019); freshwater deployments were one month (21 May–18 June 2019).
- DGT is an in-situ passive sampler for a labile/bioavailable fraction diffusing to the binding gel. It is not a total sediment digest and does not measure every contaminant class.

### 6.2 Main result and limits

The authors reported low bioavailable metal(loid) and total-Hg concentrations and MeHg below detection across deployment sites. Their interpretation was that bioavailable concentrations at the interface were low during the deployment windows, despite higher bulk sediment concentrations in some areas.

This is an important complementary result, not a contradiction of total-sediment contamination:
- Total sediment concentrations describe the inventory in the sampled solid material.
- DGT describes a time-integrated labile fraction under the deployment conditions.
- Bioavailability can change with redox, salinity, pH, organic carbon, sulfide, resuspension and season.
- The paper notes uncertainty in extrapolating DGT results to organism toxicity.

**Data-quality note:** The published DGT table as extracted contains a MeHg mean for Boat Harbour (0.00039 µg/L) that is below its listed range (0.0006–0.00082 µg/L). Do not copy this value into a dataset without checking the original table/image or contacting the authors. The table also reports differing detection limits for the marine matrix. Preserve the anomaly as a source-verification task.

## 7. Northumberland Strait — sediment and marine-biota baseline

### 7.1 Chaudhary et al. (2020)

**Paper:** “Baseline characterization of sediments and marine biota near industrial effluent discharge in Northumberland Strait, Nova Scotia, Canada.” *Marine Pollution Bulletin*, 157, 111372. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2020.111372. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32658714/

The study was designed because historical marine-biota data were inconsistent and insufficient to establish a reliable baseline.

Reported sampling design:
- Two transects from the estuary mouth, extending approximately 7 km north and 8 km northeast.
- Sixteen station positions were used for sediment/lobster/rock-crab work, divided into near-, mid- and far-field zones.
- Eight blue-mussel stations covered approximately 8 km of Pictou Harbour coastline.
- The sediment programme included 12 samples: six collected at mid/far-field stations in July 2018 using a gravity corer, and six near-field surface samples in July 2019 using a petite Ponar grab.
- Biological samples included 13 adult lobsters, 13 composite rock-crab samples and composite blue mussel samples from eight intertidal stations.
- Sediment analytes included grain size, total organic carbon, As, Cd, Cr, Cu, total Hg, Pb, Zn and dioxins/furans. Metals were analyzed by ICP-MS and dioxins/furans by high-resolution mass spectrometry.
- Selected biota analyses included metals, methylmercury and dioxins/furans.

The paper reports that sediment metal and dioxin/furan concentrations were below the CCME Interim Sediment Quality Guidelines in the sampled marine sediments. It also reports methylmercury guideline exceedances in selected lobster/rock-crab tissue samples and recommends further investigation and long-term monitoring. The article's conclusion of limited impacts/low risk is specific to the measured media, analytes, locations and sampling period.

**Interpretation:** This study is a useful near-field baseline but not a full Strait survey. It has limited sediment sample numbers, only two transects, limited coastline mussel coverage and a finite set of biological samples. It should be used as a repeatable starting set of stations, not as proof of regional absence.

### 7.2 Historical marine-biota review — Romo et al. (2019)

**Paper:** Romo, J., Chaudhary, M. & Walker, T.R. (2019). “Baseline assessment of contaminants in marine biota prior to remediation of industrial effluent impacted sediments in a former tidal estuary in Nova Scotia, Canada.” *Marine Pollution Bulletin*, 145, 641–648. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2019.06.048. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31590833/

The authors reviewed historical qualitative and quantitative marine-biota contaminant data from Boat Harbour and the surrounding marine environment. They found that species, contaminants of concern and sampling locations had often been selected ad hoc and were inconsistent with federal pulp-and-paper environmental-effects-monitoring requirements. Historical studies had measured elevated metals, dioxins and furans in marine biota, but the dataset required updating to support a defensible pre-remediation baseline.

### 7.3 American lobster baseline — Maltby et al. (2023)

**Paper:** Maltby, E.A. et al. (2023). “Baseline monitoring of contaminant concentrations in American lobster (Homarus americanus) tissues from coastal Northumberland Strait, Nova Scotia, Canada.” *Marine Pollution Bulletin*, 189, 114794. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2023.114794. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36917927/

- 2018 survey of three lobster age classes at sites 4, 20 and 70 km from the Boat Harbour estuary.
- Analytes: metal(loid)s, methylmercury, PAHs and PCDD/PCDF.
- Arsenic was 4.8–12.68 mg/kg and elevated in all age classes at all sites relative to the cited comparison.
- Mercury and methylmercury were both approximately 0.04 mg/kg; some age-class/site combinations minimally exceeded one guideline.
- No consistent pattern of accumulation by age class or at particular sites was observed.

**Interpretation:** The 4/20/70-km design provides a useful distance comparison but does not establish a contamination gradient from Boat Harbour. Total arsenic must not be treated as inorganic arsenic. A future study should use arsenic speciation if the purpose is dietary risk interpretation.

## 8. Phase II Environmental Site Assessment — what it covers and what it misses

**Source:** Supplemental Phase 2 Environmental Site Assessment PDF: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5fa9ac321d1bf94e1f13e118/t/5fb2fc6aa269ed475ca25ce7/1605565624084/Supplementary+Phase+2+Study.pdf

Reported field programme:
- 19 sediment samples from 10 site locations, plus five Northumberland Strait sediment samples.
- 20 surface-soil samples and 14 subsurface-soil samples from 13 boreholes.
- Seven groundwater samples from seven monitoring wells.
- One surface-water sample from a stream east of the sludge-disposal-cell overflow pond.
- Five background surface-water samples from Chance Harbour Lake.
- Sediment sample IDs include Northumberland Strait samples NS-SED-1 to NS-SED-7 and site locations associated with Boat Harbour, former settling ponds, the sludge disposal cell and twin settling basins.

Analyte groups included metals, mercury, PCBs, PAHs, VOCs, petroleum hydrocarbons, phenols, cyanide, chlorate/chlorite, dioxins/furans, hydrogen sulphide, grain size, fraction of organic carbon and resin/fatty acids. The report used accredited laboratory methods, including ICP-MS, GC-MS and high-resolution dioxin/furan methods as appropriate.

Important limitations and gaps:
- Ten pipeline-corridor boreholes, one hand-augered borehole and eight proposed sediment samples were not collected because of pipeline-damage risk while the facility was operating.
- Seven planned boreholes/monitoring wells around Boat Harbour and the estuary were not completed during the earlier field programme because access agreements were not obtained in time; some were later addressed in the supplemental work.
- A sample at FSP3 could not be collected below 0.7 m because underlying sediment was too dense.
- The report's sample count is not a comprehensive sample count for the entire wetland/estuary/Strait region.
- Exact concentration tables and station coordinates should be digitized and independently checked before use in a new statistical baseline.

**Required next action:** Build a machine-readable table from all ESA sample tables and laboratory certificates. Preserve the sample IDs, coordinates, depths, analytes, results, qualifiers, reporting limits and criteria. Do not rely on narrative exceedance summaries alone.

## 9. Federal Environmental Assessment — project design, predicted effects and evidence gaps

**Primary source:** Impact Assessment Agency of Canada (IAAC), *Boat Harbour Remediation Project — Environmental Assessment Report*, January 2025, Project 80164, document 71. HTML: https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/evaluations/proj/80164/document/161580?culture=en-CA. PDF: https://iaac-aeic.gc.ca/050/documents/p80164/160559E.pdf

### 9.1 Remediation concept

- Wet dredging is the preferred method for most sediment removal.
- Geotubes or equivalent dewatering are proposed.
- Mechanical excavation is anticipated in tight/shallow areas.
- Approximately 27 hectares of freshwater wetlands and 1.69 hectares of estuary were estimated to require ex-situ remediation; other areas were proposed for natural attenuation.
- The existing 6.7-hectare containment cell would be vertically expanded, with capacity increasing from 220,000 m³ to up to approximately 1,074,000 m³.
- The assessment describes approximately 180,000–188,000 m³ of existing waste in the cell and approximately 930,000 m³ of additional remediation waste in the project estimate. These are different quantities and must not be treated as a measured lagoon sediment inventory.

### 9.2 Sediment mobilization predictions

- Proponent hydraulic modelling predicted approximately 270,000 m³ of suspended solids, mainly clay, silt and sand, could be mobilized by tidal action after dam removal.
- Average daily TSS immediately after dam removal was predicted to be about ten times the applicable marine guideline threshold, declining below it within 140 days in the model.
- Modelled net deposition was approximately 4–10 cm in much of the estuary embayment.
- Armouring the estuary channel bed with gravel was modelled to reduce TSS entering the Strait by more than 50% relative to the initial scenario.
- Fisheries and Oceans Canada identified a model-domain issue that left approximately 70,000 m³ of sediment with an uncertain endpoint in an alternative scenario.

These are model results, not measured outcomes. The new independent programme should preserve model assumptions, input datasets, uncertainty bounds and predicted spatial footprints, then compare actual monitoring observations against the predictions if the project proceeds.

### 9.3 Marine baseline weaknesses identified during assessment

- Marine sediment information from 2003 was considered potentially too old by Fisheries and Oceans Canada to represent current conditions; new sediment sampling before dam removal was recommended.
- Comprehensive field-based benthic habitat information for the estuary and Northumberland Strait embayment was lacking.
- Sensitive habitat, including eelgrass, required better characterization.
- The assessment identified uncertainty about TSS equilibrium and the timing for concentrations to fall below guidelines.
- The 2020 marine baseline helps fill part of the gap but remains limited in footprint and sample count.

### 9.4 External Technical Review of containment-cell design

The BGC Engineering external technical review identified significant uncertainties in the proposed cell expansion and waste-management alternatives. Key issues included:
- Existing berm condition and slope stability had not been demonstrated sufficiently for the proposed height and side slopes.
- Global stability factors for proposed 4H:1V or 3H:1V slopes and waste heights up to about 25 m needed further demonstration.
- Geosynthetic clay liner (GCL) subgrade preparation could be affected by residual sludge/wastewater.
- End-dumped sludge in 1–3 m lifts may be difficult to compact or contain without flow.
- Geotube consolidation and differential settlement could create stress or cracking in the cover geomembrane and affect equipment bearing capacity.
- Hydrogeological water-quality mass-balance calculations relied on a single underdrain liquid sample.
- HELP modelling reportedly omitted the landfill crown and used parameters that reviewers considered inconsistent with provincial industrial landfill guidance.
- Pilot-scale leachate treatment performance did not prove full-scale performance for approximately 1 million m³ of waste; final discharge criteria and a detailed sampling/QA plan were still needed.
- Climate-related assessment was limited; cold conditions affected Geotube filling, stability and dewatering in pilot work.
- BGC recommended reconsidering contingency capacity (new on-site cell and/or off-site disposal), developing a detailed Geotube execution plan, and strengthening water monitoring/QA/QC.

These are technical-review findings and uncertainties, not proof that a containment cell failure will occur. They matter because independent monitoring should include containment-cell groundwater, surface-water, leachate and geotechnical indicators if that component proceeds.

## 15. Additional sediment and remediation-process studies recovered in the second research pass

### 15.1 Paleolimnological sediment cores — Davidson et al. (2021)

**Paper:** Davidson, K.B., Holmes, B.E.J., Spooner, I.S., Dunnington, D.W., Walker, T.R., Lake, C.B. & Su, C.-C. (2021). “Application of the paleolimnological method to assess metal contaminant distribution (As, Cu, Pb, Zn) in pulp mill stabilization basin sediments, Nova Scotia, Canada.” *Environmental Science and Pollution Research*, 28, 51342–51355. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-021-14212-x.

- The study obtained 25 lakebed sediment gravity cores between 2016 and 2019.
- It measured As, Cu, Pb and Zn and used paleolimnological methods to distinguish geogenic/background contributions from industrially influenced signals.
- The authors described more than 577,000 m³ of unconsolidated sediment in the former estuary; this is a published estimate and must not be treated as interchangeable with the 2017 paper's >170,000 m³, the existing containment-cell inventory, the projected waste volume or the modelled 270,000 m³ suspended-solids scenario.
- The authors found no distinct spatial trend in metal concentrations despite point-source effluent inputs.
- Cu and Zn variability was interpreted as reflecting both organic-rich sediment binding and pulp-mill influences; elevated Pb was attributed to atmospheric fossil-fuel combustion deposition and bioaccumulation in effluent feedstock.
- Average sedimentation was reported at approximately 1 cm per 3 years, higher than a nearby freshwater lake; the authors associated this with nutrient loading and increased productivity following effluent introduction.
- Top-sediment Zn and Cu concentrations were significantly higher, consistent with changes in effluent treatment procedures and effluent-solids composition.
- The study demonstrates the value of comparing undisturbed time-transgressive cores with pre-effluent substrate and reference sites.

**Implication:** The new field design should include dated/depth-resolved cores and background geochemistry, not only surface grabs. The absence of a distinct spatial trend in this 25-core dataset also warns against assuming a simple gradient from the discharge point.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-021-14212-x.

### 15.2 Implications for sediment-volume estimates

Published estimates include:
- >170,000 m³ of unconsolidated sediment in the 2017 Hoffman paper's context;
- >577,000 m³ in the 2020 DGT paper and 2021 paleolimnological paper;
- approximately 180,000–188,000 m³ of existing waste in the containment cell in the 2025 assessment;
- approximately 930,000 m³ of projected additional remediation waste in the containment-cell planning estimate;
- maximum containment-cell capacity up to approximately 1,074,000 m³;
- approximately 270,000 m³ of suspended solids predicted by the dam-removal hydraulic model.

These quantities refer to different sources, dates, spatial footprints and material states. They are not contradictory until the underlying definitions are aligned, and they must never be added together or treated as one measured volume.

### 15.3 Geotube dewatering and contaminant migration

A Boat Harbour sediment-specific study by Tackley, Lake and Alimohammadi (2021) examined Cu, Pb and Zn migration through geotextiles during dewatering. It found that the formation of a filter cake improved filtrate quality for the metals and particulates tested, with metals detected in filtrate primarily in solid/particulate form. The study does not validate every contaminant or full-scale treatment condition. It supports monitoring both dissolved and total fractions, TSS and process conditions throughout the dewatering train.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geotexmem.2020.09.002.


## 16. Second-pass blindspot findings — 22 November 2026

The following evidence materially expands the first review. Full analysis and limitations are in [Blindspot Closure and New Evidence](07-blindspot-closure-and-new-evidence.md).

### 16.1 Groundwater is a priority exposure pathway

The 2025 IAAC report records 2017–2018 groundwater exceedances of aluminum, arsenic, cobalt, lead and/or chloride in wells adjacent to the aeration stabilization basin. It also records that Health Canada, Nova Scotia Environment and Climate Change, and PLFN disagreed with the proponent's decision to exclude potable groundwater as an exposure pathway. The provincial regulator described the PLFN wellfield aquifer as semi-confined or "leaky" because of fracture flow; IAAC agreed the potable groundwater pathway should not have been deemed inoperable. This is evidence of a contested pathway and uncertainty, **not proof that PLFN's current wellfield is contaminated**. A hydrogeological conceptual model and independent long-term monitoring are priority requirements. [BH-029]

### 16.2 Country-food and food-web pathways require stronger scrutiny

The federal assessment records Health Canada concerns that the HHERA may have screened out relevant exposure pathways and inadequately considered bioaccumulation and biomagnification. Health Canada specifically recommended reconsidering shellfish and extending country-food monitoring beyond the estuary to address potential exposure from suspended sediment in the Strait. The Decision Statement requires follow-up monitoring for country foods, sediment, soil, air, surface water and drinking water. These are binding future programme requirements, not evidence that all such monitoring has already occurred. [BH-029, BH-035]

### 16.3 Qualify the marine baseline finding

The accessible abstract for the 2020 Northumberland Strait baseline paper reports that sediment metal, total mercury, dioxin and furan concentrations were below the cited sediment guideline thresholds, **but also reports methylmercury tissue guideline exceedances in lobster and rock crab**. The earlier summary did not make this qualification sufficiently clear. The full paper and sample-level data are needed to establish tissue type, exact concentrations, sample counts and exceedance frequency. [BH-030]

### 16.4 Existing 2016 physical-oceanography data should be recovered before duplicate surveys

The NSCC AGRG 2017 report describes 2016 topo-bathymetric lidar, seabed classification, a 35-day ADCP deployment and a hydrodynamic model of outer Pictou Harbour. It provides a valuable historical baseline but documents limitations in current-model phase/amplitude, bed resistance and habitat classification. It is not a current whole-Strait model. Recover the original digital deliverables and update them where justified. [BH-031]

### 16.5 Regional sources complicate attribution

A 2001 DFO regional habitat report identifies significant fish and shellfish habitat and historical regional pressures, including organotin/TBT contamination in Pictou and other industrial/municipal inputs. It also reports a historical estimate of 1,122 kg of mercury contributed by Canso Chemicals to the surrounding environment during 1976–1987 through liquid effluent, air, impounded solids and products. That figure is **not** a measured Boat Harbour-only discharge. It supports source-history and mass-balance research, not a claim that this mass entered the lagoon or Strait. [BH-032]

### 16.6 The 2014 pipeline spill needs a separate footprint reconstruction

ECCC confirms more than 47 million litres of untreated effluent were released in June 2014 into an area leading to East River/Pictou Harbour and that samples near the break were deleterious to fish. The enforcement notice does not establish the full spill polygon or current residual contamination. Recover the incident investigation, spill response, sampling, cleanup and follow-up records. [BH-033]

### 16.7 Dam-removal sediment predictions are consequential but remain predictions

The federal EA records the proponent's estimate of approximately 270,000 m³ of suspended solids mobilized by reintroduced tidal action, and a predicted period of elevated TSS. DFO disputed the selected guideline basis; IAAC identified model limitations and uncertainty about timing, geographic extent and benthic habitat. This is not a measured impact. Nor should the suspended-solids volume be equated with hazardous sediment volume or contaminant mass. The full model and inputs require independent review. [BH-029]

### 16.8 Updated conclusion

The first review understated the importance of groundwater, country-food exposure, marine methylmercury, existing hydrographic datasets and regional source attribution. These are now explicit work packages. They do not prove contamination beyond the known sampled footprint; they materially change what an adequate independent monitoring programme must investigate.

### 17.1 Northumberland Strait: localized sediment exceedances and major coverage gap

The 2018 Supplemental Phase II ESA reports PHC exceedances in all five sediment samples NS-SED-3 to NS-SED-7 collected at the estuary mouth/northern shoreline. Five separate background sediment samples about 2.5 km east of the estuary had no exceedances. GHD says the near-mouth PHC impact was potentially associated with mill/BHETF operations and explicitly recommends further horizontal delineation of NS-SED-1 to NS-SED-7. This is evidence of a localized near-field sediment impact and an unresolved delineation requirement; it is not proof of source attribution or a Strait-wide contamination footprint.

The same Supplemental ESA collected no new marine water-column or biota/tissue samples. Its six surface-water samples were one stream sample east of the containment-cell overflow pond and five Chance Harbour Lake background samples. Table 4.10 compiles earlier Phase II and Supplemental Phase II results and lists Northumberland Strait surface-water metals (boron/cadmium), cyanide and chloride; those entries must not be misattributed to the new Supplemental Phase II water samples. Five background sediment samples clustered 2.5 km east cannot establish conditions across the full Strait.

### 17.2 Laboratory treatability: non-detects did not always demonstrate compliance

The original 2018 GHD Laboratory Treatability Study used one source sample each from the ASB, Boat Harbour and estuary. Initial water exceeded the cited 2013 marine-water criteria for cyanide (15–21 µg/L vs 1), TPH (0.202–0.514 mg/L vs 0.1) and total/dissolved zinc (total 51.9–97.9 µg/L; dissolved 30.8–60.7 µg/L vs 10). Initial sediment PCDD/PCDF TEQ was 170 pg/g in Boat Harbour and 402 pg/g in the ASB against a 4 pg/g criterion. The report's treatment tests showed reductions, but some reporting limits exceeded the criteria: cyanide ND <10 µg/L vs 1 µg/L, mercury ND <0.2 vs 0.016, lead ND <50 vs 2, zinc ND <50 vs 10 and selenium ND <100 vs 2. These non-detects cannot establish below-criterion concentrations. Total PCDD/PCDF in BH/ASB Geotube solids remained above sediment criteria even where selected leach tests were below a chosen leachate threshold. Total solids concentration and leachability are distinct measurements.

### 17.3 Design evolution: 2018 RODD versus 2020 EIS

The 2018 RODD selected vertical expansion of the existing containment cell, full ex-situ removal of about 263,000 m³ of wetland sludge/root mass from approximately 38 ha, wet dredging with Geotube dewatering at an estimated 2,000 m³/day, low-technology on-site treatment of bulk water and dewatering effluent, off-site leachate disposal, and clean/inspect/abandon-in-place for the pipeline. The 2020 EIS changed several important elements: risk-based natural attenuation in wetlands below risk criteria with active removal of hotspots; an estimated 4,000 m³/day wet dredging rate; natural attenuation for bulk water rather than the RODD's selected low-tech treatment; temporary on-site leachate treatment during active remediation with off-site handling after closure; and full pipeline removal after PLFN's preference was incorporated. These changes require a documented decision trail, especially the wetland boundaries/volumes, bulk-water chemistry and pipeline scope.

The 2018 off-site disposal evaluation assumed a licensed provincial landfill about 55 km away, no daily load limits and a $25–$115/tonne tip fee. The 2020 EIS later said no Nova Scotia landfill within 175 km was approved to receive the anticipated dioxin/furan-impacted sludge. The RODD's community-acceptance score explicitly excluded the community near a potential receiving site, and the on-site option's social benefit included potential PLFN monitoring/O&M employment. Those were evaluation assumptions, not proof of PLFN agreement or an actual employment commitment.

### 17.4 Containment-cell and scale-up uncertainty

The 2020 EIS proposed expanding the existing 6.7 ha cell from 220,000 m³ to 1,073,000 m³ (930,000 m³ base plus 143,000 m³ contingency), with a 100-year leachate collection system and 150-year geomembrane design basis. BGC's 2021 independent review identified uncertainty in Geotube volume reduction and consolidation, potential insufficiency of even the contingency capacity, unverified slope/berm and cover-system stability, and a post-closure leachate model based on one underdrain sample from MH-1. BGC also noted that the underlying 2020 Point C water-quality trend data were not provided in the review package. These are documented design uncertainties, not proof of containment-cell failure.

BGC's summary of the 580-page GHD 2019 pilot report reports slurry solids averaging 5.2% in Phase 1 and 2.4% in Phase 2 (as low as 1–1.5%), Geotube bulking/settlement and mechanical-dredging difficulty distinguishing sludge from underlying sediment. The original GHD pilot report remains not directly opened; these pilot details are attributed to BGC's review, not a direct examination of the 580-page source.

### 17.5 Sludge mapping technology: useful physical mapping, not compound-specific chemistry

The 2021 SCG/WSP presentation describes 410 initial LIF/EC points plus 90 adaptively added points, bathymetry tied to RTK elevations, and 271,000 m³ of “competent sludge” versus 400,500 m³ of “competent sludge plus transition zone.” The 400,500 m³ is a combined total, implying about 129,500 m³ of transition zone. These figures require reconciliation against the 2020 EIS estimate of 634,000 m³ for Boat Harbour sludge/sediment; the boundaries and volume definitions may differ. LIF fluorescence is associated with PAHs/free-phase petroleum hydrocarbons and requires chemical ground truth for semi-quantitative interpretation. It cannot be treated as a universal chemical detector or proof of concentrations for metals, cyanide, dioxins/furans or other compounds.

## 18. Full-text review of the 2020 Northumberland Strait marine baseline paper — 25 November 2026

The full nine-page Chaudhary, Walker, Willis and Oakes (2020) paper has now been opened and reviewed; this supersedes the earlier abstract-only status in Section 16.3. See source BH-030.

The study sampled 16 stations on two transects extending approximately 8 km northeast and 7 km north from the estuary mouth. It analyzed 12 surficial sediment samples collected in July 2018 and July 2019, 13 adult lobster samples (including field duplicates), 13 composite rock-crab samples, and blue mussels from eight stations. Sediment and whole-body tissue were tested for metals and dioxins/furans; methylmercury was tested in selected near- and far-field tissue samples.

**Findings:**
- Sediment metals were below CCME ISQG and PEL values; total mercury in sediment was below the 0.05 mg/kg detection limit. Sediment dioxin/furan concentrations were below CCME ISQG values.
- Six whole-body lobster/rock-crab tissue samples had methylmercury concentrations of 37.3, 53.4, 41.8, 47.2, 57.5 and 39.5 ng/kg, above the 33.3 ng/kg CCME tissue-residue guideline for protection of wildlife consumers of aquatic biota.
- This 33.3 ng/kg value is a wildlife-consumer tissue-residue guideline, not a CFIA human seafood-consumption threshold. Do not imply that the paper establishes a human seafood advisory or human-health risk.
- Arsenic concentrations in lobster and rock crab exceeded the CFIA tissue guideline at sampled stations; the authors considered regional natural arsenic a likely explanation. Lead exceeded the CFIA value at one rock-crab station (CN3), with the cause unresolved.
- Blue-mussel tissue results were below the applicable tissue-residue guidelines; dioxins/furans in the three sampled marine biota species were below detection.

**Interpretation and limits:** The authors concluded there was limited evidence of industrial-effluent impact on the sampled Strait sediments/biota and low risk to marine biota, but explicitly recommended more detailed methylmercury investigation in and around Boat Harbour and periodic sediment/biota monitoring after remediation. They warned that after tidal reconnection, Northumberland Strait water flowing into Boat Harbour could potentially remobilize residual mercury. The MeHg source was not determined; the authors noted that MeHg had not previously been tested in Boat Harbour biota, water or sediment.

This is a useful near-field marine baseline, not a whole-Strait survey and not a direct assessment of the Boat Harbour basin. It does not measure the water column, suspended sediment, all fish/shellfish species, or all contaminant classes. MeHg was tested in selected tissue samples, and the raw lab certificates/sample-level data are still needed for independent reanalysis. The article disclosed funding from a Nova Scotia Lands grant and NSERC and declared no known competing interests. The disclosed funding should be retained in the provenance record; it does not by itself invalidate the peer-reviewed findings.

## 22. Lewis et al. (2020) cancer-registry analysis — full-text review and statistical interpretation — 25 November 2026

The full 12-page paper, *Governmental Fiduciary Failure in Indigenous Environmental Health Justice: The Case of Pictou Landing First Nation* (Lewis, Castleden, Apostle, Francis & Francis-Strickland, 2020), has now been opened and reviewed (BH-128).

The authors accessed the 2013 Canadian Cancer Registry microdata through the Atlantic Research Data Centre and used Statistics Canada's Standard Geographic Classification to identify cancer diagnoses among people residing in PLFN reserve subdivisions at the time of diagnosis. The PLFN dataset contained 55 cancer diagnoses from 1992–2013. The paper reports the distribution of diagnoses by broad cancer category: digestive 18%, respiratory 27%, male genital 18%, urinary 18%, and breast/female genital 9%. The corresponding comparison percentages for Nova Scotia First Nations, Pictou County, Nova Scotia and Canada are provided in BH-128. The authors report higher proportions for digestive, respiratory, male genital and urinary cancers and lower proportions for breast/female genital cancers.

This is important community-led evidence of a disparity signal and of longstanding failure to answer PLFN health concerns; it must not be dismissed, but it also must not be overstated as a causal epidemiological study. ## 23. Lewis et al. (2021) — 2014 community Environmental Health Survey — full-text review — 25 November 2026

The full nine-page *If Only They Had Accessed the Data* paper has now been opened and reviewed (BH-129). It reports the PLFN Native Women's Group-led 2014 Environmental Health Survey (EHS), developed using a Mi'kmaw environmental-health framework and Etuaptmumk/Two-Eyed Seeing. Thirteen Native Women's Group research assistants were hired; the 297-question survey generated over 400 variables; 279 people participated, a 59% response rate based on a population of 470.

## 24. Hive Engineering's 2023 alternative-site screen — source-level review

The original 23-page Hive Engineering report (4 August 2023; BH-137) has now been reviewed. It was commissioned by McKiggan Hebert Lawyers on behalf of PLFN to provide technical analysis of the IR82 alternative-containment-cell issue. It compares four locations: the existing Boat Harbour cell, Northern Pulp's active and closed landfill areas, Mount William and Granton. It also screens Crown and private parcels within 50 km.

The report is important because it directly challenges the narrow scope of the 2022 Mount William assessment. Hive says PLFN did not intend Mount William to be the only alternative and that its own work identified other parcels and a potential existing industrial landfill.

**Crown-land screening:** Hive began with 1,376 mapped locations within 50 km. It excluded 448 locations smaller than 12 acres, leaving 928 candidate areas; further GIS exclusion of mapped streams, rivers, lakes, wetlands, old growth, protected areas, significant species/habitat and potable wells reduced this to 109 parcels, ranging from 12.5 to 237 acres. Three were within 10 km; 11 between 10–20 km; 23 between 20–30 km; 37 between 30–40 km; and 34 between 40–50 km.

**Private-land screening:** the report identifies 97 candidate parcels using Service Nova Scotia Property Online visual screening and GIS layers. Hive explicitly says the list is not comprehensive because Property Online did not permit downloading the spatial data needed for a complete GIS screen; it says GeoNova files would be needed for a proper complete screening. It also says groundwater depth suitability could only be assumed and detailed field investigation was required. The 97 are therefore preliminary candidate parcels, not confirmed sites.

**Criteria:** Hive applied the criteria in the March 29, 2022 NSECC memo: Nova Scotia Municipal Solid Waste Landfill Guidelines (2004), federal National Guidelines for Hazardous Waste Landfills (PN 1365, 2006), and British Columbia's 2022 Hazardous Waste Regulation. These include 1 m separation to groundwater below the liner/leak-detection system; 100 m from permanent surface water/wetlands; 100 m from other properties; 1,000 m from buildings; surface-water and groundwater isolation; population isolation; and BC-specific requirements including 300 m from permanent waterbodies, unsaturated soil depth/permeability and geological separation from fractured/permeable bedrock.

Hive concludes that the existing Boat Harbour cell has nine criteria exceedances; Mount William has several exceedances, including water/wetland and building setbacks and population-isolation criteria; and the Northern Pulp active landfill has no exceedances against the criteria Hive applied. Hive describes Northern Pulp as the preferred site under that comparative screen. It also identifies Granton as constrained by narrow site geometry and buffers.

### What this report establishes — and what it does not

It establishes that an alternative-site desktop screen was undertaken by a consultant retained for PLFN, and that a wider candidate inventory and a comparative assessment exist. It provides a defensible reason to obtain the underlying GIS layers, parcel tables, field data and owner/permit records.

It does **not** establish that 109 Crown parcels or 97 private parcels are ready-to-build landfill sites. It does not provide the field hydrogeology, geotechnical, ecological, engineering, land-title, permitting, capacity or community-consent evidence needed for a final selection. The report's “no exceedances” for Northern Pulp means no exceedances under the selected screening criteria; it does not prove regulatory approval, present capacity, long-term containment performance or owner commitment. Hive's report is commissioned evidence and its conclusions must be attributed accordingly.

1. Obtain the complete Appendix A and B parcel tables and Appendix C four-site criteria matrix, then digitize parcel identifiers and coordinates into a GIS audit layer.
2. Obtain the underlying GeoNova parcel data and rerun the private-land screen; Hive itself says its 97-parcel list is not comprehensive.
3. Verify the Northern Pulp landfill's title, permit, active/closed cell boundaries, permitted waste types, remaining capacity, liner/leachate system, groundwater monitoring, past Boat Harbour waste acceptance and written willingness to receive the material.
4. Reconcile the exact four Mount William PIDs in Hive's report (00865485, 00865469, 65170508, 65170516) with the two parcels actually assessed in the 2022 NSECC/GHD report (00865469 and 00865485).
5. Have an independent technical reviewer apply one transparent, consistently defined criteria set to all candidate sites, including existing Boat Harbour, rather than comparing different levels of screening.

## 26. The 2014 Agreement in Principle and its implementing agreements — primary-text comparison

The original signed 2-page June 16, 2014 Agreement in Principle (BH-138) has now been reviewed against the October 14, 2014 funding MOA (BH-139), the Indian Cross Point burial-ground purchase agreement (BH-140), the Province's February 2024 IR82 response and PLFN's August 2023 response.

### What the AIP actually says

The AIP requires the Province to introduce legislation by June 30, 2015 establishing timelines for cessation of effluent reception/treatment. It separately requires the Province and Band to negotiate in good faith and execute an agreement respecting closure and remediation. The listed terms include reasonable closure timelines; reasonable costs for Band participation in negotiations; identifying and protecting Mi'kmaq burial sites/grounds at Indian Cross Point; and a conditional $1 million ex gratia payment if the Province fails to finalize the closure timeline through good-faith negotiations (unless the Band fails to negotiate in good faith). The Band agreed to dismantle/disband its blockade.

The AIP does not specify a final cleanup design, the ultimate destination of dredged waste, expansion of the existing cell, or consent to permanent storage there. Those questions should not be retroactively read into the 2014 text.

### What the two October 2014 agreements implement

**Funding MOA (BH-139):** this agreement expressly says it satisfies AIP clause 2(b), the reasonable-costs/funding provision, and no further agreement/action is required in respect of that funding requirement. It provides an accountable $100,000 advance and eligible costs for legal/technical advice, meetings, community participation, travel and communications. It is not the closure/remediation agreement described in AIP clause 2.

**Indian Cross Point purchase agreement (BH-140):** this implements part of the burial-site protection work under clause 2(c). The Province funds the Band's purchase of identified lands. The Band acknowledges meaningful and significant progress toward clause 2(c), while the Province expressly acknowledges that the purchase agreement does **not** fulfill all of its obligations under clause 2(c). It is not the overall remediation agreement.

### Why this matters to the 2024 dispute

PLFN's 2023 response says the Province did not negotiate the promised remediation agreement and that the existing-cell decision was made unilaterally. The Province's February 2024 response points to the 2014 funding MOA and burial-ground purchase agreement as evidence of continued negotiations and implementation. The actual texts show that these agreements address specific AIP components: participation costs and a defined land purchase/protection step. They do not on their face constitute the broader agreement respecting closure and remediation required by AIP clause 2.

The 2015 Boat Harbour Steering Committee TOR reproduced in GHD's EIS Appendix I describes the committee as intended to negotiate an agreement respecting closure and remediation, and lists that agreement as a deliverable. The later 2015 BHCC TOR says the cleanup agreement remained outstanding. ## 27. Indian Cross Point burial-ground investigation — Boreas 2019 archaeological/GPR report

The full 63-page Boreas Heritage Consulting report, *Indian Cross Point Archaeological Reconnaissance and Ground Penetrating Radar Survey* (September 2019; permit A2019NS064; BH-141), has now been reviewed. It was commissioned by Nova Scotia Lands to inform pipeline decommissioning and remediation near the historic Mi'kmaw burial ground at Indian Cross Point.

### What was investigated

Boreas combined desktop research (historical maps, archaeological records, local oral traditions, archival sources and environmental context) with pedestrian reconnaissance and GPR. The GPR instrument was a 500 MHz Noggin 500. Four selected grids within the pipeline right-of-way were surveyed using 25 cm transects in two directions. Those grids showed disturbance associated with pipeline installation/maintenance; two also showed the pipeline. No burial evidence was detected in those four limited ROW grids.

Adjacent-property reconnaissance identified four potential areas (PA-1 to PA-4) and one historic-use area (HA-1). A further 15 m × 11 m GPR grid (Grid 5; 106 intervals) detected scattered subsurface anomalies from approximately 70 cm depth. Boreas says several responses appeared fairly consistent in size and depth, but without excavation/ground-truthing their exact nature could not be determined. Their regularity suggested the possibility of burials.

### Historical context and findings

Boreas's archival synthesis places the historic burial ground likely on higher ground near the East River shoreline, including portions of the Palmer property, with possible extension south toward the pipeline ROW terminus. It cites George Patterson's 1877 description of rows of flat stones and human remains eroding from the bank, and Douglas Brown's 1998 research describing reinterment of remains inland after erosion. The report notes attempts to relocate the burial ground in 1995 and 1997, and the possibility that its use extends back hundreds of years or potentially to the pre-contact period. These are historical-source interpretations; exact boundaries remain unknown.

Boreas concludes:
- no burials were detected in the four GPR grids within the pipeline ROW;
- the western end of the ROW has high potential for encountering unmarked burials;
- remaining ROW sections are considered low potential;
- the shoreline area on the Palmer property and south toward the ROW terminus is the highest-potential area for locating the burial ground;
- archaeological monitoring should be present if the existing pipeline at the western end of the ROW is removed; and
- any discovery of archaeological resources or human remains requires work to stop and direction from the provincial heritage authority.

### Critical limitations and discrepancies

1. **GPR is not confirmation:** the anomalies in Grid 5 are not confirmed graves or human remains. Boreas explicitly says ground-truthing would be needed to determine their nature. No excavation/ground-truthing is reported in this assessment.
2. **Limited negative result:** “no burials detected” applies only to the four selected ROW grids, not the entire pipeline corridor, Palmer property, or Indian Cross Point.
3. **PA-3/PA-4 location inconsistency:** the executive summary says PA-4 was subjected to GPR, but the detailed body says Potential Area 3 was selected for Grid 5. The PA-4 description places it about 180 m south of the access road, while Grid 5 is described about 80 m north of the road. The actual grid location must be confirmed from original field maps/data and Boreas before attaching the anomaly result to a particular PA.
4. **Later pipeline-removal statement needs reconciliation:** IAAC's January 2025 Environmental Assessment Report says the Indian Cross Point-to-Highway 348 pipeline section was already removed by Northern Pulp, its ends capped and the area backfilled, with no adverse effects or archaeological effects identified and no further ground disturbance proposed in that corridor. This must be reconciled with Boreas's high-potential western ROW recommendation. Obtain the removal date, precise as-built limits, archaeological monitor logs, photographs, permits, stop-work/incident records and confirmation whether the high-potential section and Grid 5 anomaly area were disturbed.
5. **Burial-ground source trail:** obtain Douglas Brown's full 1998 *Indian Cross Point Burial Ground Research Final Report*, original Crown grant maps, 1995 provincial relocation records, 1997 research records, the full Baker Estate deed/transfer and any Palmer-property discussions. Exact burial locations are sensitive and should not be publicly mapped without PLFN direction.

This report is significant evidence of a known archaeological risk area and of uncertainty that remains unresolved. It is not proof that the burial ground's exact location has been found, nor proof that the pipeline removal disturbed or did not disturb remains.

## 30. February 12, 1991 Leefe letter — written provincial closure and restoration commitment

The original two-page letter from Nova Scotia Environment Minister John C. Leefe to federal Minister Thomas Siddon, dated 12 February 1991 (BH-144), has now been reviewed in its scanned form as Tab 18 of PLFN's 2014 submission.

### What the Minister confirmed

The letter states that the wastewater facility had raised Boat Harbour's water level by approximately nine feet and that this caused encroachment on three parcels of Indian Reservation land. It says the Province learned of the encroachment in September 1990.

The Minister records commitments made in November 1990:
1. Subject to the stated qualification, discontinue use of Boat Harbour as a wastewater treatment facility.
2. Return Boat Harbour's waters to their naturally tidal fluctuating regime.
3. Restore the then-exposed shoreline and alterations within Boat Harbour to their original condition, or as close as reasonably possible.
4. Complete the work in an approximate five-year period.

The letter explains that five years was needed to design, assess and construct an alternative facility to handle mill wastewater without a mill shutdown and resulting layoffs involving as many as 2,100 Scott Maritimes employees and contractors. It says the commitments were made on behalf of the Province by Robert Porter to Robert Anderson and PLFN lawyer Tony Ross. Leefe writes that the Department fully intends to honour them and expects the need for further action by Canada and PLFN to be put to rest so remedial measures can continue.

### Significance and limits

This is a contemporaneous written ministerial confirmation of provincial closure and estuary-restoration commitments. It is stronger evidence than a later recollection or summary. It is not, however, a signed agreement with PLFN, a court order or proof of completed performance. The original November 1990 meeting record, supporting land/elevation surveys, September–October 1990 correspondence and the legal form of the underlying commitment still need to be recovered.

The “approximately nine feet” statement should be checked against original survey datums, parcel boundaries and historical water-level measurements. It is a ministerial statement in a letter, not a substitute for a surveyed elevation comparison.

### Timeline to test against the original documents

- **1990–1991:** Province acknowledges encroachment and confirms a five-year plan to stop using Boat Harbour for wastewater treatment and restore tidal conditions.
- **1993:** Canada–PLFN settlement is later described by PLFN as having been reached in part on the strength of the Province's closure commitment; the settlement itself must be read to determine its actual terms and whether it incorporates or relies on the letter.
- **1995:** Province and PLFN enter a later agreement that PLFN describes as postponing closure to 31 December 2005 in exchange for interim commitments.
- **2001/2002:** proposed two-stage closure and lease extension to 2030.
- **2006:** deadline extension to 31 December 2008.
- **2008:** Province again confirms it will find another discharge location and clean up Boat Harbour.

The sequence raises a central documentary question: how the original five-year closure/restoration commitment was amended, deferred, replaced or reaffirmed by each subsequent agreement. Do not describe the later dates as a simple continuation without examining the actual operative clauses, conditions precedent, defaults, amendments and signatures.



## 31. The 1993 Canada–PLFN settlement and 1995 implementing statute — what can be verified so far

The full *Pictou Landing Indian Band Agreement Act*, S.C. 1995, c. 4 (BH-145), and the relevant House of Commons debates of 12 December 1994 and 16 February 1995 (BH-146) have now been reviewed. The signed 20 July 1993 settlement agreement itself has **not yet been independently obtained and reviewed**; the following details are therefore separated by source type.

### What the statute actually enacts

The 1995 Act defines the “agreement” as the agreement concluded on 20 July 1993 between Canada and the Pictou Landing Indian Band. Section 3 provides that payments under the agreement are not Indian moneys under the Indian Act and that subsection 35(4) does not apply. Section 4 provides that Band-member claims arising from adverse effects referred to in section 13 of the agreement may only be made against the continuing compensation account and under the procedure in that section.

The Act does **not** reproduce the agreement, set out its full release/assignment provisions, or establish that PLFN consented to permanent use of Boat Harbour. It does not impose a closure timetable on Nova Scotia or Northern Pulp.

### What the federal parliamentary record says

Government speakers described a $35 million settlement approved by a PLFN referendum of 141–25 on 5 July 1993. The described allocation was:
- $8 million for individual compensation;
- $9.725 million for a continuing compensation account for special individual claims;
- $2.275 million for community projects/general impacts; and
- $15 million for a community development trust fund intended to support relocation if needed.

The parliamentary record also says the agreement provided for environmental and health monitoring programs, that Canada undertook to explore ways to address the environmental problem, and that the settlement separated resolution of PLFN's lawsuit against Canada from decisions about the future of the Boat Harbour treatment facility. Government speakers described a release of Canada from liability for past, present and future effects and an assignment to Canada of certain possible causes of action against Nova Scotia and Scott Maritimes. One speaker expressly stated that this did not mean there could never be claims against Nova Scotia or Scott Maritimes.

### What remains unresolved

PLFN's 2014 submission says the settlement did not surrender reserve lands or interests, did not authorize indefinite effluent discharge, and assigned certain causes of action to Canada under section 12; it says PLFN disputed the validity of those assignments in its later litigation. These are PLFN's legal submissions. The 1995 statute confirms only the provisions it actually enacts.

The complete signed 1993 agreement and schedules are required to determine:
- the exact release, indemnity, assignment and subrogation language;
- the precise scope of section 13 and the continuing compensation account;
- whether and how the agreement addressed environmental/health monitoring;
- the exact federal undertaking to explore solutions;
- whether any terms address land, riparian rights or continued use of Boat Harbour; and
- how the referendum and ratification process was documented.

**Do not describe the $35 million settlement as payment for surrender of Boat Harbour or as consent to permanent effluent discharge without the actual agreement and supporting legal record.** Equally, do not state that the settlement preserved every possible PLFN claim without examining its actual release and assignment clauses. The original contract is essential.



## 32. 1996 OIC 96-621 and 1997 Evans letter — operation, water-supply and land-transfer terms

The 14 August 1996 provincial OIC 96-621 (BH-147) and 6 October 1997 letter from Greg Evans to PLFN counsel B. Anthony Ross (BH-148) have now been reviewed from the scanned attachments to PLFN's 2014 submission.

### OIC 96-621 — what it authorized

The OIC approved arrangements transferring operating responsibility for the Boat Harbour effluent treatment facility from the Province to Kimberly-Clark (successor to Scott Maritimes) for a maximum 10-year period. It also approved a further 25-year renewal of the Province–Kimberly-Clark water-supply agreement for the Abercrombie Point mill. The OIC authorized the Minister to transfer facility lands to PLFN or the federal department for the Band's benefit when no longer required for facility operation, or earlier only if the transfer would not interfere with continued operation for the agreement term and any additional time required for cleanup.

Evans writes that the Province wished to transfer the bulk of the lands to PLFN, subject to agreement on boundaries, the easement/lease required for continued operation and cleanup, continued cooperation and Governor in Council approval. The 1997 letter is a snapshot of the terms and intentions understood at that time.
