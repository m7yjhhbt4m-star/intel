## 4. Quality-control checklist for each recovered dataset

- Source organization, author, report title, date and version.
- Original file and immutable copy/hash.
- Sampling date/time and station coordinates.
- Coordinate reference system and accuracy.
- Medium, sample type, depth interval and collection method.
- Analyte, fraction/speciation, result, unit and dry/wet-weight basis.
- Detection limit, reporting limit, qualifier and uncertainty.
- Lab identity, accreditation scope and method number.
- Field/lab blanks, duplicates, spikes and reference materials.
- Guideline name, version, medium and jurisdiction.
- Whether the result is a measurement, estimate, model output or secondary-data transcription.
- Whether the sample is representative of the stratum or a targeted hotspot.
- Whether the result can be compared with other years/methods.

## 5. Stop rules

- Do not make a contamination-spread claim from a modelled plume or a single anomalous sample.
- Do not call the Strait clean based on a limited near-field baseline.
- Do not combine incompatible sediment-volume estimates.
- Do not compare concentrations without checking units, dry/wet basis, fraction and detection limit.
- Do not use a figure-derived approximate value as a precise laboratory result.
- Do not treat total arsenic as inorganic arsenic.
- Do not treat DGT-labile concentration as total sediment concentration.
- Do not treat no reported project monitoring as evidence of compliance.
- Do not finalize sampling stations before PLFN engagement, access and GIS reconstruction.

### Boat Harbour Act definition versus OIC 96-621 land-transfer scope

The consolidated *Boat Harbour Act* (BH-200) defines the “Facility” as the effluent treatment system (two settling basins, ASB, former stabilization lagoon and appurtenances) plus the mill-to-settling-basins pipeline. The containment cell is not listed in that statutory definition. Appendix L's February 2020 NS Lands email (BH-179) similarly interprets OIC 96-621 as excluding the containment cell from the “Facility” for purposes of the land-transfer authorization. However, the Act's definition and the OIC's definition are separate legal instruments. The Act's definition cannot simply be imported into OIC 96-621 without legal analysis of the OIC's exact text, Schedule A, underlying agreements, dates and property descriptions.

**Open task:** obtain a certified copy of OIC 96-621 and its Schedule A; compare the definition of “Facility” and the lands eligible for transfer with the 1995 agreements, 1996 equipment lease/licence, 2002 lease extension, containment-cell construction/approval records and land-title records. Determine whether the cell was included in the lands transferred/leased, who owns the cell parcel, and what the OIC required when the facility was no longer required for operation or cleanup. Do not state that the cell is legally excluded from all transfer obligations based solely on the 2015 Act or the 2020 email.

### Independent monitoring — commitment-to-condition timeline

The documents use “independent monitoring” for different scopes and reporting relationships. Keep them distinct:

1. **2015 government Q&A in Appendix I (BH-129):** project representatives said the Province was working with PLFN to develop an Independent Monitor and/or independent monitoring program for the life of the project, and said air and water monitoring would be continuous during cleanup and testing results would be public. These are public statements/commitments, not proof of delivery.
2. **2019 public baseline booklet (BH-134):** says a project-specific **Independent Ambient Air Monitoring and Evaluation Program** was being completed. This is specifically an air program; it does not establish independent water, sediment, benthic or biota monitoring.
3. **2020 EIS Volume V (BH-106):** identifies an Independent Ambient Air Monitoring Program as the explicit independent program; water, groundwater and marine monitoring details remain preliminary and are to be finalized with regulators, scientific advisors and PLFN. It does not define a comprehensive independent Strait-wide monitoring program.
4. **2020 draft EMP/PEPP (137872E; not yet retrieved/read):** later EIS summaries describe an Independent Monitor and Independent Air Monitor, but the original Appendix B remains inaccessible and its draft reporting line/authority must not be inferred from summaries.
5. **2025 Decision Statement and Annual Report (BH-011):** Conditions 7 and 8 require Indigenous monitors and a third-party Independent Environmental Monitor, with direct reporting to the Proponent, Mi’kmaq of Nova Scotia and IAAC and non-compliance reporting. The 2025 Annual Report says neither monitor had been retained and no specific action had occurred in the reporting year.

**Critical gap:** the 2015 promise, 2019 air program, 2020 EIS air-only program, 2020 draft EMP and 2025 Condition 8 Independent Environmental Monitor are not necessarily the same appointment or mandate. Obtain the 2017–2020 air-monitor contract/reports, final EMP/PEPP, final permits, all monitor procurement/terms of reference, funding source, sampling authority, independent lab arrangements, direct reporting rights, raw data access and public-release records. For the user's water/sediment objective, insist on independent sampling authority across the estuary, nearshore and Northumberland Strait, not merely an air-quality monitor or a monitor retained to advise the project proponent.

### HHERA / remedial-objective feasibility — explicit regulatory uncertainty

The 2025 IAAC report (BH-009) records that Health Canada believed the proponent's HHERA could underestimate risk because of excluded exposure pathways, daily exposure limits that may not fit all future land uses, and unverified assumptions. It also records the proponent's statement that recalculating SSTLs using Health Canada's recommendations could make remedial objectives technically unachievable and the project might not proceed as proposed, leaving the site risk-managed for the foreseeable future. Health Canada separately warned that exposure-point concentrations are statistical estimates and may leave hot spots; contaminated sediment could enter the Northumberland Strait after dam removal. The proponent's planned in-remediation sampling procedure is not a substitute for independent pre-remediation delineation and a transparent closure protocol.

**Priority technical review:** obtain the original HHERA model files and all supporting calculations; reproduce each exposure scenario and SSTL; run sensitivity analyses for exposure frequency/duration, receptor age, country-food consumption, shellfish, aquatic/terrestrial game, medicinal plants, potable groundwater and sediment resuspension; map the predicted residual hotspots and the post-dam-removal transport pathways; and determine whether the proposed remediation objectives are protective, achievable and independently verifiable. Do not accept “technically unachievable” as a reason to lower protection without a transparent alternative risk-management plan and PLFN agreement.

### Indian Cross Point burial-ground evidence chain (1998 → 2019 → pipeline removal)

The 1998 Douglas Brown historical research report (BH-216) reviewed archival references, Crown grants, deeds and probate records and narrowed the suspected burial-ground area sufficiently to recommend archaeological survey, but explicitly stated that no archaeological confirmation had yet occurred. ### Historical septic-condition dispute and missing 1970s monitoring records

The 2016 PLFN Victim Impact Statement reproduces three important pieces of correspondence:
- **Health Canada, circa 1970 (BH-217):** reports November/December water sampling with very high oxygen demand, concludes Boat Harbour had lost its original characteristics and was merely a retention pond, and says Fisheries and Forestry monitoring since 1967 showed progressive pollutant concentration.
- **PLFN counsel, 16 July 1974 (BH-218):** says the agreement authorized by P.C. 1966-1669 was never executed by or on behalf of Canada and argues the Province breached the remedial-action condition once a septic condition detrimental to PLFN developed.
- **Nova Scotia Attorney General, 12 February 1980 (BH-219):** says the Province had met the OIC conditions, that no septic condition existed, and that 1975–79 tests showed compliance with federal suspended-solids, BOD and toxicity requirements; it cites a 3 July 1979 Environment Canada letter saying the effluent was equal to or better than other kraft-mill treatment systems.

These are conflicting historical technical/legal positions. The underlying 1970 Health Canada sampling reports, 1967–79 Fisheries monitoring data, 1975–79 daily/monthly effluent lab certificates, toxicity test reports and the 1979 Environment Canada letter are not included in the reproduced bundle. They are priority records for determining what was actually measured, which criteria were applied, whether a “septic condition” existed, and whether the 1966 remedial condition was triggered. Do not treat either the 1980 Attorney General's assurance or the 1970 Health Canada letter as a substitute for the underlying data.
